##### Chapter 8: Reporting and Analyzing Receivables

###### Feature Story: Steering Clear of Bad Debts

- Receivables are a major asset for companies, typically the third-largest after property and inventory.
    
- NFI Group Inc. is a leading global bus manufacturer with significant accounts receivable.

- In 2018, NFI Group recorded a bad debt expense of $184 million, reflecting expected future credit losses.
    
- Accounts receivable are considered current assets, expected to be paid within about 30 days.

##### Recording and Derecognizing Notes Receivable

###### Types of Receivables

- Accounts Receivable: Amounts owed by customers from sales of goods and services, typically collected within 30 days.
    
- Notes Receivable: Formal credit instruments requiring repayment, usually with interest, for periods of 30 days or longer.
    
- Other Receivables: Nontrade receivables such as interest receivable, loans to officers, and tax recoverables.
    

###### Statement Presentation of Receivables

- Statement of Financial Position: Receivables are listed as current or non-current assets based on their due dates.
    
- Statement of Income: Receivables impact revenue recognition and may influence bad debt expense.
    

###### Principles of Sound Accounts Receivable Management

- Extending Credit: Assessing customer creditworthiness before granting credit.
    
- Establishing a Payment Period: Setting clear terms for payment to encourage timely collections.
    
- Monitoring Collections: Regularly reviewing accounts to ensure timely payments and follow-ups.
    
- Evaluating Liquidity of Receivables: Analyzing the ability to convert receivables into cash efficiently.
    

###### Learning Objective

- Identify types of receivables and record transactions related to accounts receivable.
    

###### Conclusion

- Receivables are essential financial assets representing expected cash inflows.
    
- Effective management of receivables is crucial for maintaining liquidity and financial health.
    

# Recording Accounts Receivable

##### Overview of Accounts Receivable

- Accounts receivable are recorded when performance obligations are satisfied and revenue is earned.
    
- For service companies, receivables are recorded upon service provision on account.
    
- For merchandising companies, receivables are recorded at the point of sale on account.
    

###### Initial Recording of Receivables

- Receivables are recorded at their transaction price.
    
- Transaction price is adjusted for variable consideration, including:
    
    - Expected sales returns and allowances.
        
    - Sales discounts anticipated by management.
        

###### Example: Jordache Corp. Transactions

- On January 2, Jordache sells merchandise to Polo Limited for $1,000, terms n/30.
    
- Management expects returns of 8%, with goods costing $550.
    
- On January 5, Polo returns merchandise worth $75.
    
- On January 27, Jordache receives payment for the balance due.
    

###### Journal Entries for Transactions

- January 2:
    
    - Accounts Receivable: $1,000
        
    - Sales: $920 (after adjusting for expected returns)
        
    - Refund Liability: $80 (8% of $1,000)
        
- Cost of Goods Sold: $506 (after adjusting for returns)
    
- Estimated Inventory Returns: $44
    
- January 5:
    
    - Refund Liability: $75
        
    - Accounts Receivable: $75
        
- Inventory: $42 (cost of returned merchandise)
    
- January 27:
    
    - Cash: $925 (after return adjustment)
        
    - Accounts Receivable: $925
        

###### Impact on Accounting Equation

- Assets increase by $1,000 and liabilities increase by $80.
    
- Cost of Goods Sold decreases assets by $550 and increases expenses by $506.
    
- Refund Liability decreases by $75.
    
- Inventory increases by $42.
    
- Cash increases by $925.
    

##### Cash Flow Summary

- Cash flows remain unaffected by initial receivable recording.
    
- Cash inflow of $925 occurs upon collection of accounts receivable.
    

##### Understanding the Accounting Equation and Subsidiary Ledgers

###### Accounting Equation Adjustments

- A = L + SE
    
- Adjustments:
    
    - -75: Cash Flows no effect
        
    - +42: Cash Flows no effect
        
    - +925: Cash Flows ↑ +925
        

###### Subsidiary Ledgers Overview

- General Ledger:
    
    - Provides sufficient detail for management.
        
    - Not suitable for accounts with many individual entries, like Accounts Receivable.
        
- Subsidiary Ledger:
    
    - Used to manage detailed information.
        
    - Example: Accounts Receivable subsidiary ledger for individual customer details.
        
    - Controlled by a single general ledger account known as the control account.
        
    - Balance in control account must equal total of subsidiary ledger.
        
- Importance of Subsidiary Ledgers:
    
    - Facilitates tracking of individual customer balances.
        
    - Prevents excessive detail in the general ledger.
        
    - Entries in control account must correspond with subsidiary ledger entries.
        
    - Control account often contains summary entries of detailed transactions.
        

###### Alternative Terminology

- Subsidiary ledger is also known as a subledger.
    

###### Other Accounts Supported by Subsidiary Ledgers

- Inventory: Tracks quantities and balances.
    
- Accounts Payable: Tracks individual creditor balances.
    
- Payroll: Tracks individual employee pay records.

###### Uncollectible Accounts Receivable

- Accounts receivable can become uncollectible despite credit approval.
    
- Factors for uncollectibility include economic downturns and personal financial crises.
    
- Regular review of collectibility is essential for accurate financial reporting.
    

###### Accounting Standards

- IFRS requires recognition of expected credit losses.
    
- Receivables are reported net of an allowance for expected credit losses.
    
- ASPE also mandates reporting receivables net of an allowance for doubtful accounts.
    
- Estimates are based on aging of balances, past loss experience, and current economic conditions.
    

###### Terminology

- Allowance for expected credit losses is also known as allowance for doubtful accounts.
    
- Bad Debts Expense is debited for expected credit losses from uncollectible receivables.
    

###### Recognition of Bad Debts Expense

- Bad debts expense should be recognized in the same period as the revenue from the credit sale.
    
- Delaying recognition can overstate the carrying amount of receivables.
    

###### Case Study: Quick Buck Computer Limited

- In 2020, sold 100 laptops at $400 each, totaling $40,000 in receivables.
    
- Cost per laptop was $275.
    
- In 2021, 40% of student customers defaulted on their accounts.
    
- This highlighted the risks associated with lenient credit policies.
    

##### Effects of Mismatching Bad Debts

###### Illustration Overview

- Two images representing the years 2020 and 2021.
    
- 2020: Graph shows increasing sales revenue and accounts receivable due to a huge sales promotion.
    
- 2021: Graph depicts a decline in accounts receivable and an increase in bad debts due to customer defaults.

- 2020: Overstatement of net income due to mismatched sales revenue and bad debts expense.

- Accounts receivable overstated in 2021, misrepresenting the financial position.
    

###### Allowance Method for Bad Debts

- Management estimates expected credit losses at the end of each period.
    
- Allowance recorded for expected credit losses, reflecting the difference between future cash flows and accounts receivable.
    
- Bad debts expense recorded in the same period as the sales revenue, adhering to the accrual basis of accounting.
    

###### Recording Bad Debts

- Debit Bad Debts Expense and credit Allowance for Doubtful Accounts.
    
- Allowance for Doubtful Accounts is a contra asset account, reducing the carrying amount of accounts receivable.
    
- Carrying amount reflects management’s estimate of collectible receivables after expected credit losses.
    

###### Importance of Contra Accounts

- Contra accounts are necessary as bad debts are estimates and specific customer defaults are unknown.
    
- Maintains balance between control account (Accounts Receivable) and subsidiary ledger.
    
- Helps in accurately reflecting expected credit losses in financial statements.

###### Measuring and Recording Expected Credit Losses

- Companies use various methods to estimate expected credit losses.
    
- Aging of accounts receivable is the most common method for determining allowance for doubtful accounts.
    
- Management estimates the percentage of receivables likely to be uncollectible based on:
    
    - Past credit loss experience.
        
    - Forward-looking factors (financial condition of customers, economic environment).
        
    - Industry averages for new companies.
        
- Receivables can be stratified by age, assigning different percentages to each age group.
    
- Aging improves reliability by considering balances in each age group.
    
- Companies disclose aging information in financial statement notes related to credit risk.
    

###### Decision Tool

- Users should compare percentages in each age group.
    
- Increases in older age groups indicate collection difficulties.
    

##### Aging Accounts Receivable Decision Tool

###### Overview

- Abrams Furniture Ltd. has total accounts receivable of $200,000 as of December 31.
    
- Accounts receivable are grouped by age to assess collectability.
    
- Five aging categories are used, from current (0–30 days) to over 120 days overdue.
    
- Estimated uncollectible percentages increase with the age of receivables.
    

###### Aging Schedule

- 0–30 days:
    
    - Accounts Receivable: $111,500
        
    - Estimated Percentage Uncollectible: 2%
        
    - Total Estimated Uncollectible Accounts: $2,230
        
- 31–60 days:
    
    - Accounts Receivable: $41,400
        
    - Estimated Percentage Uncollectible: 5%
        
    - Total Estimated Uncollectible Accounts: $2,070
        
- 61–90 days:
    
    - Accounts Receivable: $38,000
        
    - Estimated Percentage Uncollectible: 10%
        
    - Total Estimated Uncollectible Accounts: $3,800
        
- 91–120 days:
    
    - Accounts Receivable: $6,600
        
    - Estimated Percentage Uncollectible: 25%
        
    - Total Estimated Uncollectible Accounts: $1,650
        
- Over 120 days:
    
    - Accounts Receivable: $2,500
        
    - Estimated Percentage Uncollectible: 50%
        
    - Total Estimated Uncollectible Accounts: $1,250
        

- Total Accounts Receivable: $200,000
    
- Total Estimated Uncollectible Accounts: $11,000
    

###### Expected Credit Loss

- Total estimated uncollectible accounts represent expected credit loss.
    
- This amount is the required balance in Allowance for Doubtful Accounts.
    

###### Bad Debts Expense Calculation

- Bad debts expense is calculated as the difference between required and existing allowance balances.
    
- If the unadjusted balance is $1,000, the adjusting entry would be $10,000 to reach the required $11,000 balance.
    
- After adjustment, the carrying amount of accounts receivable is $189,000 ($200,000 - $11,000).
    

##### Carrying Amount of Accounts Receivable with Bad Debts Expenses

###### Illustration Overview

- Two T-accounts: Accounts Receivable and Allowance for Doubtful Accounts.
    
- Accounts Receivable balance: $200,000.
    
- Allowance for Doubtful Accounts transactions:
    
    - Left side: $1,000.
        
    - Right side:
        
        - Transaction 1: $10,000 (Bad Debts Expense).
            
        - Transaction 2: Balance of $11,000.
            
- Carrying amount calculated as: $200,000 - $11,000 = $189,000.
    

###### Bad Debt Adjusting Entry

- Recorded on December 31:
    
    - Debit: Bad Debts Expense $10,000.
        
    - Credit: Allowance for Doubtful Accounts $10,000.
        
- Purpose: To record estimate of uncollectible accounts.
    
- Impact on accounting equation:
    
    - Assets decrease by $10,000.
        
    - Liabilities and Shareholders' Equity remain unchanged.
        
- Cash flows: No effect.
    

###### Allowance Account with Debit Balance

- Possible to have a debit balance before adjustment.
    
- Occurs when write-offs exceed previous estimates.
    
- If a $1,000 debit balance exists:
    
    - Adjusting entry for Bad Debts Expense would be $12,000.
        
    - Adjusts allowance account to required credit balance of $11,000.
        
- Carrying amount remains $189,000 in both scenarios.
    

###### Reporting Bad Debts Expense

- Reported as an operating expense in the statement of income.
    
- Balance in Allowance for Doubtful Accounts deducted from Accounts Receivable in the statement of financial position.
    

###### Example Calculation

- Accounts Receivable: $200,000.
    
- Less: Allowance for Doubtful Accounts: $11,000.
    
- Carrying Amount: $189,000.

- An increase in the allowance as a percentage of accounts receivable indicates potential credit granting and collection issues.
    

###### Analytics Mindset

- Granting credit can enhance sales opportunities but carries the risk of unpaid receivables.
    
- Data analytics plays a crucial role in making informed credit decisions.
    
- Traditional factors for credit decisions include:
    
    - Payment history
        
    - Credit history
        
    - Debt-to-total assets ratio
        
- In the era of big data, credit decisions can incorporate numerous data points, such as:
    
    - Time spent on loan applications
        
    - Social media engagement
        
    - Spending habits
        
    - Criminal history
        
    - Utility bill information
        
    - E-commerce behavior
        
    - Online profile data
        
- Data analytics can help estimate uncollectible accounts and minimize bad debts.
    
- Companies can monitor customer payment patterns and send notifications for deviations to resolve issues early.
