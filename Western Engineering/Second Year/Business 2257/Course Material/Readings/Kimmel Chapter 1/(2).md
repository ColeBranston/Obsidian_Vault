
# Understanding Investing Activities

###### Definition

- Investing activities involve the purchase or sale of long-lived assets necessary for company operations.
    
- Assets are resources owned or controlled by a company, providing future economic benefits.
    

###### Types of Long-Lived Assets

- Property, Plant, and Equipment (PPE): Includes furniture, equipment, computers, vehicles, buildings, and land.
    
- Goodwill: Arises when a company pays more than the net identifiable assets of another company during acquisition.
    
- Intangible Assets: Non-physical assets representing rights or privileges, such as patents, copyrights, and trademarks.
    

###### Cash Flow Implications

- Investing activities typically result in cash outflows as companies invest in long-term assets.
    
- Companies may also sell long-term assets, leading to cash inflows, though usually less than outflows.
    
- Example: North West had cash outflows of $93,555 thousand for property and equipment purchases and inflows of $22,826 thousand from sales.
    

###### Investments in Other Companies

- Investing activities can include purchasing or selling shares or debt securities of other companies.
    
- These investments may generate short-term returns (dividends or interest) or serve as long-term strategic investments.
    

###### Common Misunderstandings

- Many confuse investing activities with “investments” only.
    
- The majority of investing activities relate to long-lived assets rather than securities of other companies.

##### Understanding Income and Revenue

Income, commonly known as revenue, is generated from the sale of goods and services. In accounting terms, it reflects increases in economic resources, typically through asset growth or liability reduction.

###### Sources of Income

- Sales revenue (from goods and services)
    
- Service revenue
    
- Interest income (from investments)
    
- Rental income (from unused space)
    

##### Terminology Clarification

In this context, "revenue" refers to sales from goods or services, while "income" pertains to interest, rents, dividends, and other non-operational income.

##### Alternative Terminology

- Interest income is also known as interest revenue or finance income.

###### Accounts Receivable

- Represents the right to receive cash in the future from customers.
    
- Considered an asset as it reflects future economic resources.
    

###### Types of Assets

- Long-lived assets: Purchased through investing activities (e.g., property, plant, equipment).
    
- Short-term assets: Result from operating activities (e.g., accounts receivable, supplies).
    
- Other receivables: Include interest receivable, rent receivable, and deferred tax assets.
    

###### Supplies and Inventory

- Supplies: Short-term assets for daily operations (e.g., office and cleaning supplies).
    
- Inventory: Goods held for future sale; when sold, they become an expense (cost of goods sold).
    

###### Expenses

- Defined as decreases in economic resources.
    
- Types of expenses include:
    
    - Cost of goods sold (also called cost of sales).
        
    - Selling, operating, and administrative expenses.
        
    - Interest expense (finance costs).
        
    - Income tax expense.
        
- Includes individual accounts like salaries, advertising, utilities, and depreciation.
    

###### Short-term Liabilities

- Arise from expenses, such as purchasing inventory or supplies on credit.
    
- Obligations include accounts payable, interest payable, salaries payable, and various taxes payable.
    
- Deferred tax liabilities result from accounting and tax recording differences.
    

###### Net Income

- Goal of business: Sell goods/services for a price greater than costs.
    
- Net income occurs when revenues exceed expenses.
    
- Also known as net earnings or profit.
    

###### Net Income Formula

- Revenues - Expenses = Net Income (Loss)
    

##### Net Income (Loss) Determination

North West reported net income of $90,632 thousand for the year ended January 31, 2019, as its revenues exceeded expenses. Conversely, a net loss occurs when expenses surpass revenues.

###### Summary of Business Activities

Companies engage in three types of business activities:

- **Financing Activities:**
    
    - Inflow: Issuing shares, taking out loans
        
    - Outflow: Paying dividends, repurchasing shares, repaying loans
        
- **Investing Activities:**
    
    - Inflow: Proceeds from selling long-lived assets, selling shares of other companies
        
    - Outflow: Buying long-lived assets, purchasing shares of other companies
        
- **Operating Activities:**
    
    - Inflow: Revenues, collection of receivables, sale of services or goods
        
    - Outflow: Expenses, payment of payables, purchase of inventory and supplies
        

###### Details of Business Activities

Financing activities involve:

- Equity financing: Issuing shares, paying dividends, repurchasing shares
    
- Debt financing: Borrowing cash, repaying debt
    

Investing activities include:

- Purchasing and disposing of long-lived assets
    
- Long-term investments
    

Operating activities encompass:

- Day-to-day operations
    
- Revenues and expenses
    
- Changes in accounts: receivables, supplies, inventory, payables

###### Financial Statements Overview

The purpose and content of each financial statement include:

- **Balance Sheet:** Displays assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific point in time.
    
- **Income Statement:** Shows revenues and expenses over a period, resulting in net income or loss.
    
- **Cash Flow Statement:** Reports cash inflows and outflows from operating, investing, and financing activities.
    
- **Statement of Changes in Equity:** Details changes in equity accounts over a period.
    

##### Operating Activity Outflow: Expense (Utilities Expense) and Financial Statements

###### Purpose of Financial Statements

- Provide information for internal and external users.
    
- Help assess financial position, performance, and capital structure.
    

###### Main Financial Statements

- **Statement of Income:** Reports revenues and expenses over a period.
    
- **Statement of Changes in Equity:** Shows changes in shareholders’ equity components over time.
    
- **Statement of Financial Position:** Displays assets, liabilities, and shareholders’ equity at a specific date.
    
- **Statement of Cash Flows:** Illustrates cash inflows and outflows during a period.
    

###### Additional Information

- Notes to financial statements provide clarifications and details.
    
- Essential for understanding financial performance and position.
    

###### Other Financial Statements

- Statement of Comprehensive Income for other comprehensive income items.
    
- Private corporations may use a Statement of Retained Earnings instead of a Statement of Changes in Equity.
    

###### Reporting Frequency

- Public corporations must produce financial statements annually and quarterly.
    
- Monthly statements may be prepared for internal use.
    
- A fiscal year is typically one year in length.
    

## What Is A Fiscal Year

- A fiscal year is the 12 months a company or government chooses to coincide with planning, budgeting, or revenue cycles.
    

###### Fiscal Year Ends

- Companies may choose different fiscal year ends based on operational cycles.
    
- Examples of fiscal year ends include:
    
    - Lululemon: Sunday closest to January 31
        
    - Jean Coutu: Saturday closest to February 29 or March 1
        
    - Canadian Tire: Saturday closest to December 31
        
    - Lions Gate Entertainment: March 31
        
    - Shaw Communications: August 31
        
- Most government entities use March 31 as their fiscal year end.

##### Sierra Corporation Statement of Changes in Equity

###### Overview

- Shows changes in total shareholders’ equity for a specific period.
    
- Begins with account balances at the start and ends with balances at the close of the period.
    
- Time period aligns with the statement of income (year, quarter, or month).
    

###### Components of Shareholders' Equity

- Shareholders’ equity includes:
    
    - Share capital
        
    - Retained earnings
        
    - Other accounts (e.g., accumulated other comprehensive income)
        

###### Share Capital

- Represents amounts received from shareholders upon initial share issuance.
    
- All companies must have common shares; some may also have preferred shares.
    
- Calculation of changes in share capital:
    
    - Common shares, beginning of period
        
    - + Common shares issued (sold)
        
    - - Common shares repurchased
        
    - = Common shares, end of period
        

###### Retained Earnings

- Cumulative net income retained in the corporation, not distributed as dividends.
    
- Negative retained earnings indicate a deficit (net losses exceed net income).
    
- Calculation of changes in retained earnings:
    
    - Revenues - Expenses = Net Income (or Loss)
        
    - Retained earnings, beginning of period + Net income (or Loss) - Dividends declared = Retained earnings, end of period
        

###### Dividends

- Dividends are distributions of retained earnings to shareholders.
    
- Not reported as an expense in the statement of income.

##### Statement of Financial Position

- Reports assets and claims to those assets at a specific point in time.
    
- Claims are divided into liabilities (creditors) and shareholders’ equity (owners).
    

###### Basic Accounting Equation

- Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity
    

##### Understanding the Basic Accounting Equation

###### Basic Equation

- Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity
    
- Reflects the balance sheet concept.
    
- Assets must equal claims from creditors and shareholders.
    

###### Financing of Assets

- Assets financed through:
    
    - Debt (borrowed from creditors)
        
    - Equity (investments from shareholders)
        
    - Retained earnings (net income retained in the company)

##### Sierra Corporation Statement of Cash Flows Overview

###### Main Function

- Provides financial information about cash receipts and payments for a specific period.
    
- Helps investors, creditors, and others analyze a company’s cash position.
    
- Reports effects on cash from:
    
    - Operating activities
        
    - Investing activities
        
    - Financing activities
        

###### Types of Activities

- **Operating Activities:** Transactions that create revenues and expenses.
    
- **Investing Activities:** Purchase or sale of long-lived resources (e.g., property, equipment) and long-term securities.
    
- **Financing Activities:** Borrowing or repaying long-term debt, issuing or repurchasing shares, and distributing dividends.
    

###### Presentation Order

- Operating activities are presented first.
    
- Followed by investing activities.
    
- Financing activities are presented last.
