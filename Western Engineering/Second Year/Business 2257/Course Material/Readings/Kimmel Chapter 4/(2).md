
# Adjusting Entries Overview

## Purpose of Adjusting Entries

- Update accounts at the end of the accounting period.
    
- Ensure asset and liability accounts are reported accurately.
    
- Record revenues in the period they are earned.
    
- Record expenses in the period they are incurred.
    

## Need for Adjusting Entries

- The trial balance may not contain complete and up-to-date data due to:
    
    - Events not recorded daily for practicality (e.g., supplies usage, employee salaries).
        
    - Costs incurred over time, recorded at period end (e.g., rent, insurance, depreciation).
        
    - Unrecorded items due to unknown amounts (e.g., utility bills received after the period).
        

## Preparation of Adjusting Entries

- Requires understanding of company operations and account interrelationships.
    
- May involve counting supplies and preparing supporting schedules for contracts.
    
- Information for adjustments often gathered after the accounting period ends.
    

## Trial Balance Analysis

- Each account in the trial balance must be analyzed for completeness.
    
- Trial balance before adjustments is called an unadjusted trial balance.
    

## Frequency of Adjusting Entries

- Required every time financial statements are prepared:
    
    - Public corporations: at least quarterly.
        
    - Private corporations: at least annually.
        
    - Monthly financial statements require monthly adjusting entries.
        

## Classification of Adjusting Entries

- Adjusting entries can be classified into two main categories:
    
    - **Prepayments:**
        
        - Prepaid expenses: Expenses paid in cash and recorded as assets before use.
            
        - Deferred revenues: Cash received and recorded as liabilities before revenue is earned.
            
    - **Accruals:**
        
        - Accrued expenses: Expenses incurred for which cash has yet to be paid.
            
        - Accrued revenues: Revenues earned for which cash has yet to be received.
            

###### Subcategories of Prepayments and Accruals

- Prepaid expenses
    
- Accrued expenses
    
- Deferred revenues
    
- Accrued revenues
    

##### Accrued Revenues Overview

Accrued revenues refer to revenues that have been earned but for which cash has not yet been received or recorded through journal entries.

###### Adjusting Entries for Prepayments

Learning Objective: Prepare adjusting entries for prepayments.

- Steps of the accounting cycle include analyzing, journalizing, posting, and preparing trial balances.
    
- Adjusting entries for prepayments or accruals are crucial for accurate financial reporting.
    

##### Understanding Prepayments in the Accounting Cycle

###### Introduction to Prepayments

- Prepayments are costs paid in advance, such as a 12-month insurance policy.
    
- They increase current assets (e.g., prepaid expenses) and can affect non-current assets.
    
- Expenses are recognized monthly rather than at the time of cash payment.
    

###### Alternative Terminology

- Prepayments are also referred to as deferrals.
    

###### Customer Prepayments

- Companies can receive prepayments from customers, increasing current liabilities (e.g., deferred revenue).
    
- Revenue is recognized monthly as services are provided, not when cash is received.
    

###### Prepaid Expenses

- Prepaid expenses are payments for expenses benefiting more than one accounting period.
    
- Examples include insurance, supplies, advertising, and rent.
    
- Prepaid expenses are recorded as assets until they expire or are used.
    

###### Adjusting Entries for Prepaid Expenses

- Adjusting entries are made at each statement date to:
    
    - Record the reduction of the asset (Prepaid Expenses).
        
    - Record the corresponding expense for the current accounting period.
        

###### Helpful Hint

- A cost is an asset if it has future benefits; it is an expense if benefits have expired.
    

###### Journal Entries

- Initial entry for prepaid expenses: debit Prepaid Expenses, credit Cash.
    
- Adjusting entry: debit Expense account, credit Prepaid Expenses account.
    

##### Adjusting Entries for Prepaid Expenses

###### Types of Journal Entries

- **Transaction Journal Entry:**
    
    - Debit Prepaid Expenses (Asset) with a positive sign.
        
    - Credit Cash (Asset) with a negative sign.
        
- **Adjusting Journal Entry:**
    
    - Debit Expenses with a positive sign.
        
    - Credit Prepaid Expenses with a negative sign.
        

###### Impact of Unadjusted Prepaid Expenses

- Assets are overstated.
    
- Expenses are understated.
    
- Net income, retained earnings, and shareholders’ equity are overstated.
    

###### Example: Insurance

- Insurance protects against losses (fire, theft, unforeseen events).
    
- Insurance is typically paid in advance for annual coverage.
    
- Payments recorded in the Prepaid Insurance asset account.
    
- Adjustments needed at financial statement date:
    
    - Debit Insurance Expense for the cost used.
        
    - Credit Prepaid Insurance for the same amount.
        

###### Case Study: Sierra Corporation

- Paid $600 for a one-year insurance policy on October 5.
    
- Coverage period: October 1 to September 30 of the following year.
    
- Original transaction recorded as:
    
    - Debit Prepaid Insurance: $600
        
    - Credit Cash: $600
        
- Balance in Prepaid Insurance account as of October 31: $600.
    
- Monthly insurance expense: $50 ($600 ÷ 12 months).
    

###### Adjusting Entry Analysis

- Adjusting entries follow the debit-credit rules established in prior chapters.

##### Prepaid Expenses for Insurance

###### Adjustment Overview

- Adjustment on October 31: $50 of insurance expired.
    
- Insurance cost calculated as $600 divided by 12 months.
    

###### Steps Involved

- **Basic Analysis:**
    
    - Decrease in Prepaid Insurance (asset) by $50.
        
    - Increase in Insurance Expense (expense) by $50.
        
    - Decrease in shareholders’ equity by $50.
        
- **Equation Analysis:**
    
    - Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity.
        
    - Prepaid Insurance: -$50 under Assets.
        
    - Insurance Expense: -$50 under Retained Earnings.
        
    - No changes under Liabilities.
        
- **Debit-Credit Analysis:**
    
    - Debit Insurance Expense $50 (increases expenses).
        
    - Credit Prepaid Insurance $50 (decreases assets).
        
- **Journal Entry:**
    
    - Date: October 31.
        
    - Debit: Insurance Expense $50.
        
    - Credit: Prepaid Insurance $50.
        
    - Description: To record insurance expired.
        
- **Posting:**
    
    - Prepaid Insurance T-account:
        
        - Beginning balance: $600 (debit).
            
        - Adjustment: $550 (debit).
            
    - Insurance Expense T-account:
        
        - Posting: $50 (credit).
            

###### Post-Adjustment Balances

- Prepaid Insurance balance: $550 (remaining 11 months of coverage).
    
- Insurance Expense balance: $50 (cost used in October).
    
- Without adjustment, October expenses would be understated and net income overstated by $50.
    
- Assets and shareholders’ equity would also be overstated by $50.
    

##### Supplies Purchase

###### Transaction Overview

- Supplies purchased on October 9 for $2,500.
    
- Increase in Supplies (asset) account.
    
- Supplies Expense recognized at the end of the accounting period.
    

###### Accounting Treatment

- Remaining supplies counted at period end.
    
- Difference between Supplies account balance and actual supplies on hand equals supplies used (expense).
    

###### Original Transaction Recording

- Date: October 9.
    
- Debit: Supplies $2,500.
    
- Credit: Accounts Payable $2,500 (liability).
    

###### Accounting Equation Impact

- Assets increase by $2,500.
    
- Liabilities increase by $2,500.
    
- No effect on cash flow at the time of purchase.
    

##### Adjustment of Supplies and Supplies Expense

###### Initial Transaction on October 9

- Supplies purchased: $2,500
    
- Accounts Payable: $2,500
    
- Cash Flow: No effect
    

###### October 31 Supplies Count

- Supplies on hand: $1,000
    
- Cost of supplies used: $1,500 ($2,500 - $1,000)
    

###### Adjustment Process

- Reduce Supplies account by $1,500
    
- Increase Supplies Expense account by $1,500
    

###### Steps of Adjustment

- **Basic Analysis:**
    
    - Decrease Supplies (asset) by $1,500
        
    - Increase Supplies Expense (expense) by $1,500
        
    - Decrease Shareholders’ Equity by $1,500
        
- **Equation Analysis:**
    
    - Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity
        
    - Supplies: -$1,500
        
    - Supplies Expense: -$1,500
        
- **Debit-Credit Analysis:**
    
    - Debit Supplies Expense: $1,500
        
    - Credit Supplies: $1,500
        
- **Journal Entry:**
    
    - Date: October 31
        
    - Debit Supplies Expense: $1,500
        
    - Credit Supplies: $1,500
        
    - Description: To record Supplies used
        
- **Posting:**
    
    - Supplies T-account:
        
        - Beginning balance (Oct 9): $2,500
            
        - Adjustment (Oct 31): -$1,500
            
        - Ending balance: $1,000
            
    - Supplies Expense T-account:
        
        - Adjustment (Oct 31): $1,500
            

###### Post-Adjustment Balances

- Supplies account balance: $1,000
    
- Supplies Expense account balance: $1,500
    

###### Consequences of Not Making Adjustment

- Supplies will be overstated
    
- Supplies Expense will be understated
    
- Overstatement of assets, net income, retained earnings, and shareholders’ equity
    

##### Understanding Depreciation

- Depreciation is the allocation of the cost of long-lived assets over their useful life.
    
- Long-lived assets include buildings and equipment, which provide economic benefits over time.
    
- The useful life is the period during which these assets generate revenue.
    
- Only assets with finite useful lives are depreciated; assets with indefinite lives, like land, are not.
    
- Depreciation is a cost allocation concept, not a valuation concept.
    

###### Calculation of Depreciation

- The straight-line method is a common approach for calculating depreciation.
    
- Annual depreciation is calculated by dividing the asset's cost by its useful life.
    
- Example: For equipment costing $5,000 with a useful life of 5 years, annual depreciation is $1,000.
    
- Depreciation can be adjusted for partial years; for example, monthly depreciation for the equipment is approximately $83.
    

###### Example of Depreciation Calculation

- Sierra Corporation purchased equipment for $5,000 on October 1.
    
- Annual depreciation: $5,000 ÷ 5 years = $1,000.
    
- Monthly depreciation: $1,000 ÷ 12 months = approximately $83.
    

###### Important Notes

- Depreciation is an estimate and should be calculated to the nearest month rather than day.
    
- Using days for calculation may lead to inaccuracies.
    

##### Illustration of Prepaid Expenses and Depreciation

###### Adjustment Overview

- Adjustment Date: October 31
    
- Depreciation Amount: $83
    
- Calculation: $5,000 / 5 * 1/2
    

###### Five Steps of Depreciation Accounting

- **Basic Analysis:**
    
    - Increases Accumulated Depreciation (contra asset) by $83
        
    - Decreases total assets by $83
        
    - Increases Depreciation Expense by $83
        
    - Decreases shareholders’ equity by $83
        
- **Equation Analysis:**
    
    - Accounting Equation: Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity
        
    - Accumulated Depreciation listed under Assets
        
    - Cash and Depreciation Expense shown as negative $83 under Shareholders’ Equity
        
    - No items under Liabilities
        
- **Debit-Credit Analysis:**
    
    - Debit Depreciation Expense: $83 (increases expenses)
        
    - Credit Accumulated Depreciation Equipment: $83 (increases contra assets)
        
- **Journal Entry:**
    
    - Date: October 31
        
    - Debit: Depreciation Expense $83
        
    - Credit: Accumulated Depreciation Equipment $83
        
    - Description: To record monthly used
        
- **Posting:**
    
    - Equipment T-account:
        
        - Beginning Balance (Oct 1): $5,000 (debit)
            
        - Posting (Oct 31): $5,000
            
    - Accumulated Depreciation-Equipment T-account:
        
        - Beginning Balance (Oct 31): $83 (credit)
            
        - Posting (Oct 31): $83
            
    - Depreciation Expense T-account:
        
        - Posting (Oct 31): $83 (credit)
            
        - Posting (Oct 31): $83
            

###### Understanding Contra Accounts

- Depreciation Expense is debited (increased)
    
- Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment is credited (increased)
    
- Accumulated Depreciation is a contra asset account
    
- Normal balance of contra accounts is opposite to related asset accounts
    

###### Helpful Hint

- Every contra account has a normal balance opposite to that of the account it relates to.
    

##### Understanding Contra Accounts and Depreciation

###### Definition of Contra Accounts

- Contra accounts have a normal balance opposite to the related account.
    
- Example: Accumulated Depreciation is a contra account to Equipment.
    

###### Purpose of Using Contra Accounts

- Separates actual costs from estimated costs.
    
- Discloses original cost and total depreciation expensed to date.
    
- Helps assess the relative age of an asset by comparing cost and accumulated depreciation.
    

###### Accumulated Depreciation Details

- Increases by $83 monthly ($1,000 annually) until fully depreciated in five years.
    
- Represents cumulative total of depreciation expense since asset purchase.
    
- Equipment account balance remains unchanged at $5,000 unless equipment is sold or purchased.
    

###### Impact of Not Making Adjusting Entries

- Depreciation expense will be understated.
    
- Net income will be overstated by $83.
    
- Total assets and shareholders’ equity will also be overstated by $83.
    

###### Statement Presentation of Depreciation

- Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment is deducted from the Equipment account.
    
- Example presentation:
    
    - Equipment: $5,000
        
    - Less: Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment: $83
        
    - Carrying Amount: $4,917
        

###### Carrying Amount Explanation

- Difference between cost of a depreciable asset and its accumulated depreciation.
    
- Also known as net book value or book value.
    
- Purpose of depreciation is to allocate cost over time, not to state asset value.
