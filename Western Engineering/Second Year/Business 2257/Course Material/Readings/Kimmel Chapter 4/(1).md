
##### Accrual Accounting Concepts

###### Feature Story: School’s Out, Time to Balance the Books

At Western University in London, Ontario, the academic year aligns with the fiscal year, ending on April 30. This synchronization aids in recognizing revenues and expenses accurately.

- Intersession courses: Fees are collected before April 30, but revenue is deferred until services are provided in May.
    
- Residence admission fees: Collected in advance but recognized in September when students move in.
    
- Research grants: Funds received in March are deferred until expenses are incurred, matching revenue with related costs.
    
- Expenses: Must be recorded in the year incurred, including post-employment benefits, utility bills, vacation pay, and salaries.
    

Accrual accounting is preferred as it provides a more accurate financial picture by recognizing revenues and expenses in the period they relate to, regardless of cash transactions.

##### Accrual Versus Cash Basis of Accounting

###### Accrual Basis Accounting

- Revenues are recorded when goods or services are provided, not when cash is received.
    
- Expenses are recorded in the period they benefit, rather than when they are paid.
    
- Transactions are recorded in the periods in which events occur.
    
- Revenues are recognized when earned; expenses are recognized when incurred.
    
- Reflects changes in economic resources (assets and liabilities) regardless of cash flow timing.
    

###### Cash Basis Accounting

- Revenues are recorded only when cash is received.
    
- Expenses are recorded only when cash is paid.
    
- Appealing due to simplicity, but can lead to misleading information.
    
- Timing differences can misrepresent financial performance (e.g., sales on account).
    

###### Comparison Example: Colours Paint Ltd.

- Year 1:
    
    - Expenses incurred and paid: $50,000.
        
    - Customer billed: $80,000 (payment received in Year 2).
        
- Accrual Basis Results:
    
    - Year 1: Revenue: $80,000; Expense: $50,000; Net Income: $30,000.
        
    - Year 2: Revenue: $0; Expense: $0; Net Income: $0.
        
    - Combined: Revenue: $80,000; Expense: $50,000; Net Income: $30,000.
        
- Cash Basis Results:
    
    - Year 1: Revenue: $0; Expense: $50,000; Net Income: -$50,000.
        
    - Year 2: Revenue: $80,000; Expense: $0; Net Income: $80,000.
        
    - Combined: Revenue: $80,000; Expense: $50,000; Net Income: $30,000.
        

###### Helpful Hint

- Accrual Basis: Revenues recognized when earned; expenses recognized when incurred.
    
- Cash Basis: Revenues recognized when cash is received; expenses recognized when cash is paid.
    

##### Accounting Matters

###### Accrual Basis of Accounting

- Revenue is recorded when earned, typically when services are performed.
    
- Expenses are recorded when incurred to generate revenue.
    
- Net income for Year 1 is $30,000; no revenue or expenses reported in Year 2.
    

###### Cash Basis of Accounting

- Only expenses are recorded in Year 1 when paid.
    
- Only revenues are recorded in Year 2 when received.
    
- Net loss in Year 1 is $50,000; net income in Year 2 is $80,000.
    

###### Comparison of Net Income

- Total net income over two years is the same at $30,000.
    
- Year 1 shows a net loss of $50,000; Year 2 shows a net income of $80,000.
    
- Cash basis does not provide a clear picture of financial performance for each year.
    

###### Importance of Accrual Accounting

- Accrual accounting is preferred for financial reporting.
    
- Revenues and expenses are recognized in the period they relate to, regardless of cash flow.
    
- Provides a more meaningful picture of financial position and operations.
    

###### Differences Between Accrual and Cash Basis

- **Revenue Recognition:**
    
    - Accrual: When earned (service performed or goods delivered).
        
    - Cash: When cash is received.
        
- **Expense Recognition:**
    
    - Accrual: When incurred to generate revenue (service used or goods consumed).
        
    - Cash: When cash is paid.
        

###### Regulatory Standards

- Accrual basis is required for financial statement preparation.
    
- Not permitted under IFRS or ASPE to use cash basis accounting.
    

# Understanding Accounting Methods in Universities

##### Key Insights on Accounting Practices

- Timely decision-making is crucial for universities.
    
- Cash basis accounting may delay awareness of financial issues.
    
- Accrual basis accounting provides a clearer financial picture sooner.
    

###### Scenario Overview

- University receives $1 million in tuition at the start of a four-month term.
    
- Monthly expenses total $300,000, leading to $1.2 million over four months.
    
- Critical to know the university is operating at a loss.
    

###### Accrual vs. Cash Basis Accounting

- Accrual Basis:
    
    - Revenue recognized monthly: $250,000.
        
    - Loss identified at the end of the first month.
        
    - Allows for proactive financial management.
        
- Cash Basis:
    
    - Initial revenue reported as $1 million.
        
    - Net income appears as $700,000 initially.
        
    - Delays realization of financial losses until later months.
        

###### Example: Jomerans Ltd. Revenue Calculation

- December 31, 2020: Customers owed $30,000 for prior services.
    
- 2021 Cash Collected: $125,000, including $30,000 for 2020 services.
    
- December 31, 2021: Customers owed $19,500 for 2021 services.
    

###### Revenue Calculation Results

- Cash Basis Revenue: $125,000
    
- Accrual Basis Revenue Calculation:
    
    - Cash received: $125,000
        
    - Less: 2020 receivables collected: ($30,000)
        
    - Add: Amounts owed at year-end: $19,500
        
    - Total Accrual Basis Revenue: $114,500
        

##### Amounts Owing by Customers and Revenue Recognition

- Amounts owing by customers at December 31, 2021: $19,500
    
- Revenue using the accrual basis of accounting: $114,500
    

###### Revenue Recognition Principles

- Income is recognized when there is an increase in economic resources.
    
- Revenue is often used interchangeably with income.
    
- Revenue arises from ordinary activities, such as:
    
    - Provision of services in service companies.
        
    - Sale of merchandise in merchandising companies.
        
- Income from non-ordinary activities includes:
    
    - Gains from the sale of land.
        
    - Investment income (interest, rent, dividends).
        

###### Variability in Income Types

- Different companies may classify similar income differently.
    
- Example: Interest received may be termed Interest Revenue or Interest Income based on the company's primary activities.
    

###### Revenue Sources at Western University

- Tuition and student fees.
    
- Government and research grants.
    
- Sales from bookstore and retail shops.
    
- Parking passes and rental income from residences.
    
- Donations and investment returns.
    

###### Revenue Recognition Standards

- ASPE (Accounting Standards for Private Enterprises):
    
    - Revenues recognized when earned.
        
    - Three conditions must be met:
        
        - Services provided or risks/rewards of ownership transferred.
            
        - Revenue amount must be reliably measurable.
            
        - Collection must be reasonably assured.
            
- IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards):
    
    - Revenues recognized when rights in a contract increase or obligations decrease.
        
    - Performance obligations must be satisfied.
        
    - Utilizes a five-step model for revenue recognition.
        

# Revenue Recognition Process for Service Revenue

## Overview of the Five Steps

- Identify the contract with the client or customer.
    
- Identify the performance obligations in the contract.
    
- Determine the transaction price.
    
- Allocate the transaction price to the performance obligations in the contract.
    
- Recognize revenue when (or as) the company satisfies the performance obligation.
    

## Step 1: Identify the Contract

- Analyze contract details as a source document.
    
- Includes terms, price, rights, and obligations of both parties.
    
- Must have commercial substance affecting cash flows.
    
- Evaluate the probability of collecting the transaction price.
    

## Step 2: Identify Performance Obligations

- Determine the services to be provided to the customer.
    
- Can involve a single or multiple distinct performance obligations.
    
- Services are distinct if they can benefit the customer separately.
    

## Step 3: Determine the Transaction Price

- Identify the expected amount of consideration for services.
    
- Reflect any discounts, refunds, or rebates (variable consideration).
    

## Step 4: Allocate the Transaction Price

- Allocate the transaction price to identified performance obligations.
    
- Use stand-alone selling prices for each obligation.
    
- Calculate the combined stand-alone selling price.
    
- Allocate based on the percentage of each obligation's price to the total.
    

## Step 5: Recognize Revenue

- Revenue is recognized as each performance obligation is satisfied.
    
- When a service is provided, recognize the allocated revenue.
    

##### Decision Tool

Record revenue in the period in which the performance obligation is satisfied.

# Revenue Recognition Decision Tool

Record revenue in the period in which the performance obligation is satisfied.

## General Principles

- Revenue is recognized when the performance obligation is satisfied.
    
- In service companies, revenue is recognized when the service is performed.
    
- In merchandising companies, revenue is recognized at the point of sale.
    
- Revenue is recorded regardless of cash receipt timing.
    

## Example: Wajax Ltd.

Wajax Ltd. signed a contract for $2,200 to provide:

- One-day software training course in September.
    
- Five hours of follow-up training in October.
    

Normal charges are:

- $1,875 for the one-day training course.
    
- $125 per hour for follow-up training.
    

### Five-Step Revenue Recognition Process

#### Step 1: Identify the Contract

- Contract includes terms of service, pricing ($2,200), and payment terms.
    
- Collectibility is reasonably certain.
    

#### Step 2: Identify Performance Obligations

- One-day software training course in September.
    
- Five hours of follow-up training in October.
    

#### Step 3: Determine Transaction Price

- Total transaction price is $2,200.
    

#### Step 4: Allocate Transaction Price

- Allocate based on relative stand-alone selling prices:
    
    - Software training course: $1,875 (75%) → $1,650
        
    - Follow-up training: $625 (25%) → $550
        
- Combined stand-alone selling price: $2,500.
    

#### Step 5: Recognize Revenue

- Recognize $1,650 in September for the software training course.
    
- Recognize $550 in October for the follow-up training.

###### Expense Recognition

- Expenses recognized when there is a decrease in economic resources.
    
- Linked to revenue recognition; expenses matched with revenues in the same period.
    
- Expenses recognized when incurred, regardless of cash payment.
    

###### Decision Tool

- Expenses should match revenues, aligning effort with results.
    

##### Understanding Expense Recognition in Relation to Revenues

###### Key Principles

- Expenses should align with revenues, matching effort with results.
    
- Expenses lead to changes in assets and liabilities.
    
- Expenses decrease future economic resources, unlike revenues which increase them.
    
- Dividends to shareholders are not classified as expenses.
    

###### Example: Wajax Training Services

- Wajax provided software training services in September.
    
- Employees earned salaries, creating a liability (Salaries Payable) and an expense (Salaries Expense).
    
- Expenses were recognized in the same month as revenues due to simultaneous occurrence.
    

###### Challenges in Expense Recognition

- Not all expenses can be directly linked to revenue.
    
- Monthly rent increases liabilities (Rent Payable) and expenses (Rent Expense) regardless of revenue earned.
    
- Interest expenses may not directly correlate with revenue generation.
    
- Depreciation of long-lived assets affects revenue generation over multiple periods.
    

###### DO IT! 4.1d: Expense Recognition Transactions

- Employees earned salaries of $4,700: **Expense recognized** (Salaries Expense).
    
- Paid $6,300 for supplies used immediately: **Expense recognized** (Supplies Expense).
    
- Incurred interest charges of $1,100 on a bank loan: **Expense recognized** (Interest Expense).
    
- Received an invoice for $1,900 in natural gas costs: **Expense recognized** (Utility Expense).
    
- Purchased equipment for $10,000: **No expense recognized** (Asset acquisition).
    
- Paid $2,700 for advertising for next month: **No expense recognized** (Prepaid Expense).
    
- Declared dividends of $6,500: **No expense recognized** (Distribution to shareholders).
    

###### Action Plan for Expense Recognition

- Evaluate if an asset has been consumed or a liability created during revenue-generating activities.
    
- Remember that distributions to shareholders do not count as expenses.
