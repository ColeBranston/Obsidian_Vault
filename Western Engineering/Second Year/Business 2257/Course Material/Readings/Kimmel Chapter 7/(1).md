
# Internal Control and Cash

##### Control Activities

###### Importance of Cash Control

- Cash is essential for business operations and must be managed carefully.
    
- Failure to manage cash can lead to bankruptcy, even for successful companies.
    

###### Components of a Good Internal Control System

- Establishing clear procedures for cash handling.
    
- Implementing technology to monitor cash transactions.
    
- Regular audits and cash counts to ensure accuracy.
    

###### Limitations of Internal Control

- Internal controls can be circumvented by collusion among employees.
    
- Human error can lead to inaccuracies in cash handling.
    
- Cost of implementing controls may outweigh benefits for smaller businesses.
    

###### Fraud Prevention

- Segregation of duties to limit access to cash.
    
- Authorization required for exceptions, such as voiding sales.
    
- Use of POS systems to track transactions and reduce fraud risk.
    

###### Cash Management Practices

- Regular deposits to the bank to minimize cash on hand.
    
- Storing excess cash in a safe to prevent theft.
    
- Monitoring cash flow to ensure sufficient liquidity for operations.
    

###### Reporting Cash in Financial Statements

- Cash is reported as a current asset on the balance sheet.
    
- Disclosure of cash handling risks in financial statements.

- Specific control activities vary based on risk assessment and company size.

    - Assignment of responsibility

##### Assignment of Responsibility in Internal Control

###### Overview

- Internal control requires assigning responsibility to specific employees.
    
- Responsibilities can be assigned by employee level, e.g., only managers can approve discounts.
    
- Effective when one employee or level is authorized for a specific task.
    
- Management must define activities for each employee or manager.
    

###### Illustration

- Example: Each cashier has a separate cash drawer.
    
- If cash is missing, it’s clear who is responsible.
    
- Multiple users of the same drawer complicate accountability.
    
- Secure cash drawers represent a physical control activity.
    

###### Authority in Control Systems

- Proper authority is crucial for developing internal control systems.
    
- Example: The vice-president of finance should set credit sales policies.
    
- Sales motivations may conflict with credit policies.

##### Segregation of Duties

###### Importance of Segregation of Duties

- Essential for internal control systems.
    
- No single employee should handle authorization, recording, and custody of assets.
    
- Reduces potential for fraud and misappropriation of assets.
    

###### Fraud Risks

- Fraud involves intentional misappropriation or misstatement of financial information.
    
- Combining responsibilities increases opportunities for fraudulent activities.
    

###### Application in Purchasing Activities

- Different employees should handle:
    
    - Ordering goods or services
        
    - Approving orders
        
    - Receiving goods
        
    - Authorizing payments
        
- Risks of a single employee handling all purchasing tasks:
    
    - Receiving bribes to inflate prices.
        
    - Creating fictitious invoices.
        
    - Stealing merchandise.
        
- Segregation lowers fraud opportunities and unintentional errors.
    

###### Application in Sales Activities

- Sales-related tasks must also be segregated:
    
    - Approving customer credit
        
    - Shipping goods
        
    - Preparing and recording invoices
        
- Potential abuses include:
    
    - Unauthorized pricing by salespersons.
        
    - Shipping goods to oneself.
        
    - Understating invoices for friends and relatives.
        
- Segregation helps prevent these abuses.
    

###### Challenges for Small Businesses

- Fewer employees make segregation difficult.
    
- Owner involvement is crucial for oversight.
    
- Examples of owner involvement:
    
    - Making daily bank deposits.
        
    - Reviewing monthly bank reconciliations against daily sales reports.
        

##### Understanding Assignment of Responsibility vs. Segregation of Duties

###### Key Concepts

- **Assignment of Responsibility:** Specific employees are held accountable for their actions.
    
- **Segregation of Duties:** Duties are spread across multiple employees to prevent any single employee from having complete control over an asset.
    

###### Importance of Distinction

- Helps in establishing accountability within the organization.
    
- Reduces the risk of fraud and errors by ensuring checks and balances.
    

###### Implementation Strategies

- **Unique Employee Identification:** Assign unique employee numbers for transaction recording to track individual actions.
    
- **Role Distribution:** Ensure that different employees handle various aspects of a transaction.
    

###### Example Scenario

- Multiple purchasing clerks initiate purchases using their unique employee numbers (assignment of responsibility).
    
- The purchasing manager approves the purchases (segregation of duties).
    
- Receiving clerks in the warehouse take custody of the purchased assets (segregation of duties).
    

###### Conclusion

- Both control activities work together to enhance internal controls.
    
- Understanding their differences is crucial for effective risk management.
    

# Inventory Control Issues at Sports Equipment Business

##### The Missing Control

###### Overview

- Tim Chan is the operations manager at a sports equipment distribution centre.
    
- The company uses a perpetual inventory system to track inventory levels.
    
- Tim delegated monthly inventory counts to a distribution clerk.
    
- External auditors discovered discrepancies in inventory counts during year-end audits.
    

###### Segregation of Duties

- The distribution clerk had access to physical inventory and was responsible for counts.
    
- This arrangement allowed potential theft and inaccurate reporting.
    
- Tim was advised to perform counts due to his lack of involvement in warehouse operations.
    

###### Documentation

- Documentation is crucial for tracking transactions and inventory.
    
- Sequentially prenumbered documents help prevent duplicate or missing entries.
    
- Original documents should be forwarded to the accounting department promptly.
    
- Photocopies increase the risk of errors in transaction recording.
    

# Inventory Control Issues at Sofas and More Ltd.

##### The Missing Control

###### Overview

- Sofas and More Ltd. operates furniture stores in Eastern Canada.
    
- Products are ordered from overseas and received at a warehouse in Halifax.
    
- Supplier invoices are used to update inventory records.
    
- Receiving employees do not complete paperwork for received goods.
    
- Product shortages have occurred despite inventory records indicating items are in stock.
    

###### Management Concerns

- Uncertainty exists regarding potential theft from stores or warehouses.
    
- Management is unsure if suppliers are billing for goods not received.
    

##### The Missing Control

###### Documentation

- Employees receiving goods must complete receiving reports.
    
- Reports should indicate type, quantity, and condition of goods.
    
- Receiving reports must be prenumbered.
    
- Completed reports are sent to the accounting department.
    
- Reports are used to update inventory and verify supplier invoices.
    
- This process prevents payment for goods not received.
    

###### Internal Control Activities

- Documentation is identified as the missing control activity.
    
- Employees should sign receiving reports for accountability.
    
- Inventory record changes are made by accounting staff to ensure segregation of duties.
    
- Comparison of receiving reports with supplier invoices is essential for review and reconciliation.
    

###### Physical Controls

- Physical controls safeguard assets and enhance accounting accuracy.
    
- Examples of physical controls include:
    
    - Safes and vaults for cash and documents.
        
    - Locked warehouses for inventory and records.
        
    - Computer facilities requiring secure access (passwords, biometrics).
        
    - Alarms to prevent unauthorized access.
        
    - Security cameras and sensors to deter theft.
        
    - Time-tracking systems for employee attendance.
        
- Assets should also be adequately insured against theft and damage.
    

###### Missing in Action Scenario

- Monique and Chantal work at a bar and sometimes share shifts.
    
- Employees must swipe cards to register arrival and departure times.
    
- Monique often arrives late, and Chantal swipes her card.
    
- Chantal sometimes leaves early and asks Monique to swipe her card later.
    
- This practice has been ongoing for over eight months.
    

##### The Missing Control

###### Physical Controls

- Time-tracking systems should be easily observable.
    
- Aim to prevent payroll fraud (e.g., “punching in or out” for others).
    
- Without this control, employers risk paying for unworked hours.
    

###### Review and Reconciliation

- Four key control activities:
    
    - Assignment of responsibility
        
    - Segregation of duties
        
    - Documentation
        
    - Physical controls
        
- Reviews should be independent, conducted by someone not involved in the activity.
    
- Necessary to prevent forgetfulness or intentional neglect of controls.
    
- Reconciliations involve comparing documents (e.g., receiving reports with sales invoices).
    

###### Internal Reviews

- Conducted by employees or managers to ensure asset security.
    
- Recommended measures for effective internal reviews:
    
    - Regular and surprise reviews.
        
    - Independence of the reviewer from the personnel responsible for the information.
        
    - Reporting discrepancies to management for corrective action.
        
- Internal auditors evaluate the effectiveness of internal controls.
    
- Public companies must include management reports in annual reports, detailing internal control responsibilities.
    
- CEO and CFO certifications regarding internal control effectiveness are required.
    
- Material weaknesses must be reported in the Management Discussion and Analysis (MD&A) section of the annual report.
    

##### Internal Controls and Reporting Requirements

- Companies must report material weaknesses in internal controls over financial reporting.
    
- This information is included in the Management Discussion and Analysis section of the annual report.
    

##### External Reviews

- External auditors are independent professionals hired by the board of directors.
    
- They assess whether financial statements fairly present the company's financial position.
    
- All public companies, including The North West Company, are required to undergo external audits.
    
- External auditors evaluate the company's internal control systems related to financial reporting.
    
- Deficiencies identified are reported to management and the board.
    
- The independent audit committee oversees the adequacy of internal control systems.

##### Limitations of Internal Control

No matter how well it is designed and operated, a company’s system of internal control can only provide reasonable assurance that assets are properly safeguarded and that the accounting records are accurate and reliable. This is due to several limitations:

- Cost/Benefit Considerations
    
- Human Error
    
- Collusion
    
- Management Override
    

###### Cost/Benefit Considerations

- The costs of establishing control activities should not exceed the expected benefits.
    
- Management may choose not to implement controls if benefits are less than costs.
    
- Example: Retail stores may avoid hiring security guards due to high costs and negative customer impact.
    
- Less costly procedures may be used, such as:
    
    - Posting warning signs.
        
    - Using hidden cameras and store detectives.
        
    - Implementing sensor equipment at exits.
        
- In small businesses, limited staff may lead to inadequate segregation of duties.
    
- Example: A cashier may handle cash and prepare bank deposits, increasing risk of fraud.
    

###### Human Error

- Human error can limit the effectiveness of internal control systems.
    
- Causes of human error include:
    
    - Lack of training.
        
    - Employee fatigue.
        
    - Carelessness.
        
    - Indifference.
        
- Example: A poorly trained receiving clerk may inaccurately count goods, affecting inventory records.
    

###### Collusion

- Collusion occurs when two or more individuals work together to bypass control activities.
    
- This undermines the effectiveness of internal controls by negating the benefits of duty segregation.
    
- Example: A supervisor and cashier may collaborate to understate cash receipts and steal funds.
    
- Collusion represents a form of fraud that cannot be entirely eliminated by internal controls.
    

# Management Override

Management has the authority to override internal controls, which can lead to fraud. This can occur when:

- Managers ignore internal control policies.
    
- Overrides are made for personal gain or to manipulate financial results.
    

Regular assessments by the audit committee and auditors are crucial to monitor management's actions and responsibilities. A strong commitment to internal control is essential for establishing a positive control environment.

## Fraud

Fraud involves intentional actions to misappropriate assets or misstate financial information. Key characteristics include:

- Intentional or deliberate actions distinguish fraud from errors.
    
- Fraud is likely when the following factors are present:
    

###### Fraud Triangle

- **Opportunity:** The ability to commit fraud.
    
- **Pressure:** Motivation or incentive to commit fraud.
    
- **Rationalization:** Justification for the fraudulent behavior.
    

##### Understanding the Fraud Triangle

###### Key Elements of the Fraud Triangle

- **Opportunity:**
    
    - Fraud occurs when employees perceive insufficient internal controls.
        
    - Inadequate monitoring can lead to theft and a belief that they won't be caught.
        
- **Pressure:**
    
    - Personal financial issues, such as debt, can drive employees to commit fraud.
        
    - Desire for an unaffordable lifestyle or performance targets can also create pressure.
        
    - Motivation can stem from family expectations or the challenge of "beating the system."
        
- **Rationalization:**
    
    - Employees justify their actions by feeling underpaid or believing the company is wealthy.
        
    - They may think they will repay the company or that others are doing the same.
        

###### Internal Controls to Mitigate Fraud

- **Preventive Controls:**
    
    - Examples include requiring dual signatures on checks.
        
- **Detective Controls:**
    
    - Examples include bank reconciliations to identify fraudulent activities.
        
    - Detective controls help prevent future fraud by increasing the likelihood of detection.
        

###### Types of Fraud

- **Employee Fraud:**
    
    - Management fraud is the most serious, as management can override internal controls.
        
    - Such frauds are harder to detect compared to non-management fraud.
        
- **Third-Party Fraud:**
    
    - Suppliers may submit duplicate invoices or charge for unperformed work.
        
    - Customers may engage in shoplifting or incorrect scanning at self-checkouts.
