
### Understanding Financial Statements for Investment Decisions

###### Importance of Financial Statements

- Financial statements provide critical information for potential investors.
    
- Investors assess short-term and long-term investment opportunities.
    
- Key metrics include net income, liquidity, solvency, and profitability ratios.
    

###### Case Study: Aritzia Inc.

- Aritzia is a leading women’s clothing retailer in Canada and the U.S.
    
- Founded in 1984 by Brian Hill, it has over 92 stores and an online presence.
    
- Expanded into the U.S. market in 2005 with the help of Berkshire Partners.
    
- Took the company public in 2016, with Hill and Berkshire owning a majority of voting shares.
    

###### Key Financial Metrics

- Basic Earnings Per Share (EPS): Measures income earned on each common share.
    
- Price-Earnings (P-E) Ratio: Indicates how much investors are willing to pay for each dollar of earnings.
    
- As of March 3, 2019, Aritzia had a net income of $79 million, with an EPS of $0.70 and a P-E ratio of 23.2.
    
- A higher EPS is more attractive to investors.
    

###### Market Capitalization

- Market capitalization reflects the total value of a company's issued common shares.
    
- Aritzia's market capitalization was $1.9 billion at the end of fiscal 2019, classifying it as a "small cap" stock.
    
- Small cap companies often have greater growth potential compared to large cap companies.
    
- Aritzia continues to expand, opening about six new stores annually, with a history of never closing a store.

###### The Classified Statement of Financial Position

The statement of financial position provides a snapshot of a company's financial status, detailing assets, liabilities, and shareholders' equity at a specific time. It organizes these elements into standard classifications to enhance understanding.

- **Sections of the Statement**
    
    - **Assets**
        
        - Current Assets
            
        - Non-current Assets
            
    - **Liabilities**
        
        - Current Liabilities
            
        - Non-current Liabilities
            
    - **Shareholders’ Equity**
        
- **Purpose of Classifications**
    
    - Assessing the ability to pay liabilities as they come due.
        
    - Understanding claims of creditors on total assets.

##### Understanding Assets

Assets are resources owned or controlled by a company that provide future economic benefits through their ability to generate cash flows. They can generate benefits directly or indirectly. Assets are categorized as:

- **Current Assets:** Benefits realized within one year.
    
- **Non-Current Assets:** Benefits realized over more than one year.
    

###### Current Assets

Current assets are expected to be converted into cash, sold, or used up within one year from the financial statement date or the operating cycle, whichever is longer. The operating cycle is the average time taken to:

- Pay cash for products or services.
    
- Receive cash from customers for those products or services.
    

In service businesses, the cycle includes:

- Performing services for customers on account.
    
- Paying employees for those services.
    
- Collecting cash from customers.
    

While most businesses have an operating cycle of up to one year, some, like vineyards or aircraft manufacturers, may have longer cycles. For this context, a one-year timeframe is assumed for classifying assets.

###### Common Types of Current Assets

Common types of current assets include:

- Cash and cash equivalents
    
- Accounts receivable
    
- Inventory
    
- Prepaid expenses
    

##### Common Types of Current Assets

###### Overview of Current Assets

- Current assets are resources expected to be converted into cash within one year.
    
- Includes cash, trading investments, accounts receivable, notes receivable, inventory, supplies, and prepaid expenses.
    

###### 1. Cash

- Includes cash on hand and cash in bank accounts.
    

###### 2. Trading Investments

- Investments in debt or equity securities intended for short-term resale.
    
- Aims to earn income from price fluctuations.
    

###### 3. Accounts Receivable

- Amounts owed by customers for products or services purchased on credit.
    
- Supported by sales invoices; may include accrued receivables for interest, taxes, and rent.
    

###### 4. Notes Receivable

- Amounts owed by customers or others, backed by a written promise to repay.
    
- Includes loans receivable; typically bears interest.
    

###### 5. Inventory

- Goods held for sale to customers, often referred to as merchandise.
    
- Retailers sell inventory directly to consumers.
    

###### 6. Supplies

- Consumable items like office and cleaning supplies not intended for sale.
    
- Expected to be used within the year.
    

###### 7. Prepaid Expenses

- Costs paid in advance for future benefits, such as rent and insurance.
    
- Reflects unused benefits available for future use during the year.
    

###### Reporting Current Assets

- No prescribed order for reporting current assets; typically listed by liquidity in North America.
    
- Real estate companies in Canada may list in reverse order of liquidity.

###### Cash Equivalents

- Short-term, highly liquid investments with minimal risk.
    
- Can be easily sold; further details in Chapter 7.
    

##### Overview of Non-Current Assets

Non-current assets, also referred to as long-term assets, are those that a company does not plan to convert into cash, sell, or utilize within one year from the financial statement date or its operating cycle. They encompass all assets that are not classified as current assets.

###### Alternative Terminology

- Non-current and long-term are used interchangeably.
    

###### Common Types of Non-Current Assets

- Long-term investments
    
- Property, plant, and equipment
    
- Intangible assets
    
- Goodwill
    
- Other assets
    

###### Long-Term Investments

Long-term investments, also known simply as investments, include:

- Multi-year investments in debt securities (e.g., loans, notes, bonds, mortgages) intended to earn interest.
    
- Equity securities (e.g., shares) of other companies held for many years for investment income or strategic purposes.
    

These assets are classified as non-current because they are not easily marketable or because management does not intend to sell them within one year. The term "investments" without modifiers typically refers to non-current assets.

###### Property, Plant, and Equipment Overview

- Tangible assets with long useful lives used in business operations.
    
- Includes land, buildings, equipment, furniture, and vehicles.
    
- Presented in order of permanency:
    
    - Land (indefinite life)
        
    - Buildings
        
    - Equipment
        

###### Cost Model vs. Revaluation Model

- Most companies use the cost model to record property, plant, and equipment.
    
- Some may use the revaluation model to show fair value, but this is rare.
    

###### Depreciation of Assets

- Costs allocated to expense over estimated useful lives through depreciation.
    
- Depreciation calculated by assigning a portion of the asset’s cost each year.
    
- Land is not depreciated due to its indefinite life.
    

###### Presentation of Depreciable Assets

- Cost of asset less accumulated depreciation shown on the statement of financial position.
    
- Accumulated depreciation is a contra asset account.
    
- Difference between cost and accumulated depreciation referred to as carrying amount (or net book value).

###### Intangible Assets

- Definition:
    
    - Assets with no physical substance but significant value.
        
    - Considered non-current assets providing benefits for more than one year.
        
- Examples:
    
    - Patents
        
    - Copyrights
        
    - Franchises
        
    - Trademarks
        
    - Trade names
        
    - Licences
        
- Classification:
    
    - Finite Useful Lives: Amortized over their useful lives.
        
    - Indefinite Useful Lives: Not amortized.
        

###### Accounting Terminology

- IFRS:
    
    - Depreciation for tangible assets.
        
    - Amortization for intangible assets with definite lives.
        
- ASPE:
    
    - Amortization for both tangible and intangible assets with definite lives.
        
- Interchangeable Use:
    
    - Some companies use depreciation and amortization interchangeably.
        

###### Goodwill

- Definition:
    
    - Asset from acquiring another company at a price higher than the fair value of net identifiable assets.
        
- Calculation:
    
    - Goodwill = Price Paid - Fair Value of Net Identifiable Assets.
        
- Characteristics:
    
    - No physical substance.
        
    - Cannot be sold separately from the company.
        
    - Reported separately from other intangibles.

##### Liabilities Overview

- Present obligations from past transactions.
    
- Result in the transfer of economic resources.
    
- Obligations arise from transactions with other parties.
    

###### Classification of Liabilities

- **Current Liabilities:** Due within one year or operating cycle.
    
- **Non-current Liabilities:** Due after more than one year.
    

###### Current Liabilities Examples

- Accounts payable
    
- Short-term loans
    
- Accrued expenses
    
- Other obligations due within one year
    

##### Overview of Current Liabilities

###### Bank Indebtedness

- Short-term loan from a bank.
    
- Used to cover cash shortfalls via an operating line of credit.
    

###### Accounts Payable

- Amounts owed to suppliers for credit purchases.
    
- Recorded upon receipt of an invoice.
    
- Includes other payables such as salaries, interest, sales tax, rent, and income tax.
    
- Estimated amounts for payables without invoices are referred to as accrued payables.
    

###### Deferred Revenue

- Cash received from customers before delivering goods or services.
    
- Recorded as a current liability until the service is performed.
    
- Can be classified as a non-current liability if the revenue will not be earned within one year.
    

###### Notes Payable

- Written promises to pay amounts owed, often to banks or suppliers.
    
- Typically bear interest, unlike accounts payable unless overdue.
    
- Includes bank loans, which are often referred to interchangeably with notes payable.
    
- Can be classified as current or non-current liabilities.
    

###### Current Portion of Long-Term Debt

- Represents the portion of long-term loans due within the next year.
    
- Includes notes, bank loans, and mortgages.
    
- The remaining portion is classified as a non-current liability.
    

###### Presentation of Current Liabilities

- North American companies typically list current liabilities by order of liquidity.
    
- Some companies may use internal customs for arrangement.
    
- Real estate and international companies may list liabilities in reverse order of liquidity.
    

###### Example from Cogeco Communications Inc.

- Bank Indebtedness: $5,949
    
- Trade and Other Payables: $328,693
    
- Income Tax Liabilities: $16,133
    
- Deferred Revenue: $67,699
    
- Current Portion of Long-Term Debt: $77,188
    
- Total Current Assets: $495,662
    

###### Importance of Current Liabilities

- Users of financial statements analyze the relationship between current assets and current liabilities.
    
- This relationship is crucial for evaluating a company's ability to meet its current obligations.
    

##### Non-Current Liabilities Overview

Non-current liabilities, also known as long-term liabilities, are obligations expected to be settled after one year. Examples include:

- Notes payable (bank loans, mortgages, bonds)
    
- Lease liabilities
    
- Pension and benefit obligations
    
- Deferred liabilities
    

###### Types of Non-Current Liabilities

- **Notes Payable:** Can be current or non-current, depending on maturity.
    
- **Mortgages Payable:** Long-term notes secured by property.
    
- **Bonds Payable:** Issued by corporations/governments, traded in active markets.
    
- **Lease Liabilities:** Future payments on long-term rental contracts.
    
- **Pension and Benefit Obligations:** Retirement benefits owed to employees.
    
- **Deferred Income Tax Liabilities:** Arise from accounting and tax treatment differences.
    
- **Deferred Revenue:** Income not earned within a year.
    

###### Reporting Non-Current Liabilities

Companies provide detailed notes in financial statements regarding non-current liabilities, including:

- Maturity date
    
- Interest rate
    
- Future payment schedules
    
- Assets pledged as security
    

###### TELUS Corporation Example

As of December 31, 2018, TELUS Corporation reported non-current liabilities totaling $17,833 million, broken down as follows:

- **Long-term debt:** $13,265 million
    
- **Other long-term liabilities:** $1,466 million
    
- **Deferred income taxes:** $3,152 million
    

###### Additional Details

TELUS provides further information in its financial statement notes, including:

- Liabilities related to legal claims
    
- Employee termination costs
    
- Obligations for ceasing use of property, plant, and equipment
    
- Details on long-term debt, including amounts, interest rates, and maturity dates
    
- Other long-term liabilities, such as pensions and post-retirement benefits
    

##### Shareholders’ Equity Overview

Shareholders’ equity represents the residual interest in a company's assets after liabilities are deducted. It consists of:

- **Share Capital**
    
- **Retained Earnings**
    

Some companies may also include:

- Accumulated other comprehensive income
    
- Contributed surplus
    

##### Understanding Share Capital

###### Definition

- Share capital represents the total value of shares issued by a company.
    
- Shareholders invest cash or other assets to purchase shares.
    
- Ownership certificates are issued in the form of common or preferred shares.
    

###### Types of Shares

- Common Shares: Typically the only class of shares issued by many companies.
    
- Preferred Shares: Issued in addition to common shares, providing different rights and privileges.
    

###### Classification

- All classes of shares issued are collectively referred to as share capital.
    
- In some regions, common shares are referred to as ordinary shares.
    

###### Alternative Terminology

- Share capital is also known as capital stock.
