
-> Showing the useful nature of Green's theorem in various applications.

**Problem Statement:**
$$\text{Evaluate}:\oint_{C_+} \left[ (x^5 + 3y) \, dx + (2x - e^{y^3}) \, dy \right]\tag{1}$$

for C a circle of radius 2, centered at (x,y) = (1,5).

![[Pasted image 20250203094934.png]]
**Figure 1: Contour and associated integration region for first example.**


The equation for C thus defined is (schematic in Figure 1):

$$(x-1)^2 + (y-5)^2 = 4\tag{2}$$

This time, let’s first consider how we would do this by line integration directly1. One way we could do this is to divide the circle into a top-half and a bottom half, and define y(x) separately for each (see Figure 2). Then we could integrate each one as a 1-D integration along x between-1 and 3 (different directions for top versus bottom). Let’s just try the top-half and see what the integral starts to look like.

![[Pasted image 20250203095143.png]]
**Figure 2: Parameterizations for upper and lower line integrals composing the circle.**

If you don’t immediately see how we arrived at those equations for y(x), consider the following progression. First, equation for a circle of radius 2, centered at (0,0):

$$y^2+x^2 = 4\tag{3}$$

Next, shift the circle to the right by 1 and solve for $y$:

$$
y^2 + (x - 1)^2 = 4 \tag{4}
$$

$$
y = \pm \sqrt{4 - (x - 1)^2}\tag{5}
$$

Now shift both functions up by 5:
$$
y = \pm \sqrt{4 - (x - 1)^2} + 5 \tag{6}
$$
So the equation for the top half of the circle is:
$$
y = \sqrt{4 - (x - 1)^2} + 5\tag{7}
$$
and the bottom half would be:

$$
y = -\sqrt{4 - (x - 1)^2} + 5
$$
Okay, so we have y(x) (just taking top half for the moment, which would be the integral along C1). Now we need dy:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx} \left( [4 - (x - 1)^2]^{1/2} + 5 \right) \quad (9)
$$

$$
= \frac{1}{2} [4 - (x - 1)^2]^{-1/2} (-2(x - 1)) \quad (10)
$$
So $dy$ would be:
$$
dy = \frac{1 - x}{\sqrt{4 - (x - 1)^2}} \, dx
$$
Now we have to assemble the line integral C1 parameterized by *x*:

$$
\int_{C_1} = \int_{3}^{-1} \left[ (x^5 + 3(\sqrt{4 - (x - 1)^2} + 5)) \right] dx+\tag{12}
$$

$$
\left[ (2x - \exp(\sqrt{4 - (x - 1)^2} + 5))^3 \right] \left( \frac{1 - x}{\sqrt{4 - (x - 1)^2}} \right) dx\tag{13}
$$

So yeah, that’s pretty bad. You can probably sense that this would be easier in polar coordinates, which is true. But it would not be easy. The limits on r as a function of θ would be pretty complicated also (and not easy to obtain). So all in all, this is a terrible integral (and this is just the top-half- you would need to do it again, in slightly different form, for the bottom portion of the line). Help!

- Green’s Theorem to the rescue! Let’s take a shot at this with Green’s Theorem. Recalling standard form for Green’s Theorem in the plane:

$$
\oint_{C_+} [P \, dx + Q \, dy] = \iint_{R} \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right) dA \tag{14}
$$

Inspecting (1) we see that we have:

$$
P(x, y) = (x^5 + 3y), \quad \implies \quad \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 3, \tag{15}
$$
$$
Q(x, y) = (2x - e^{y^3}), \quad \implies \quad \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = 2. \tag{16}
$$
Therefore

$$
\oint_{C_+} [P \, dx + Q \, dy] = \iint_R (2 - 3) \, dA \tag{17}
$$

$$
= (-1) \iint_R \, dA \tag{18}
$$

$$
= -4\pi \tag{19}
$$

where we have noted in going from (18) to (19) that the area of a circle of radius 2 is simply 4π.

- Clearly this problem was highly contrived in order to make the line integration virtually impossible, but the double integration virtually trivial. This is the kind of situation where Green’s Theorem is in a sense at its most powerful.

**Example 2: "Green's Theorem is not limited to Cartesian Coordinates**

- **Problem Statement:** Find the work done by ⃗ F acting along the simple closed curve C figure in Figure 3.

$$\vec{F}=(-16y+sin(x^2))\hat{i}+(4e^y+3x^2)\hat{j}$$

![[Pasted image 20250203100444.png]]
**Figure 3: Contour and associated integration region for first example.**

- When you are calculating work around a closed curve, you should be tempted to think: “if this vector field is conservative, this integral is going to be zero”. You should maybe be tempted to ask yourself that for every closed contour integral you are ever faced with! But at this point, you have probably already absorbed the fact that the statement ⃗ F is conservative is the same as the integrand of the double integral in Green’s Theorem being zero (because the integrand is the perpendicular component of the Curl, and saying a vector field is conservative and the Curl of that vector field is zero are the same thing). So we’ll just proceed to using Green’s Theorem here, and if the integrand turns out to be zero, we know ⃗ F is conservative (and the line integral is also zero).

Just to be utterly complete about this, the work done by ⃗ F around C is of course:

$$
W = \oint_{C_+} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = \oint_{C_+} \left( -16y + \sin(x^2) \right) dx + \left( 4e^y + 3x^2 \right) dy \tag{21}
$$

Identifying the component functions2 (we do not have to always call these P and Q, but it’s helpful when we’re starting out to just keep the nomenclature very steady, very repetitive...):

$$
P(x, y) = -16y + \sin(x^2), \quad \implies \quad \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = -16, \quad \text{(22)}
$$

$$
Q(x, y) = 4e^y + 3x^2, \quad \implies \quad \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = 6x. \quad \text{(23)}
$$
So the work integral would be:

$$
W = \iint_R (6x + 16) \, dA\tag{24}
$$

If you go back to Figure 3, and just say that we’re going to do a double integral over R, well, this is clearly a case where we would expect polar coordinates to be the way to go. The limits on θ are constants (π/4 to 3π/4). The limits on r are constants (r from 0 to 1). So let’s set this up in polar coordinates

$$x=rcos\theta, y=rsin\theta, dA=r\;dr\;d\theta\tag{25}$$

So our work integral becomes:

$$
W = \int_{\pi/4}^{3\pi/4} \int_{0}^{1} (6r \cdot \cos \theta + 16) r \, dr \, d\theta \quad \text{(26)}
$$

$$
= \int_{\pi/4}^{3\pi/4} \int_{0}^{1} (6r^2 \cdot \cos \theta + 16r) \, dr \, d\theta \quad \text{(27)}
$$

$$
= \int_{\pi/4}^{3\pi/4} \left( 2r^3 \cdot \cos \theta + 8r^2 \right) \bigg|_{0}^{1} \, d\theta \quad \text{(28)}
$$

$$
= \int_{\pi/4}^{3\pi/4} (2 \cdot \cos \theta + 8) \, d\theta \quad \text{(29)}
$$

$$
= \left[ 2 \cdot \sin \theta + 8\theta \right] \bigg|_{\pi/4}^{3\pi/4} \quad \text{(30)}
$$

$$
= 2 \cdot \sin \frac{3\pi}{4} + 8 \left( \frac{3\pi}{4} \right) - 2 \cdot \sin \frac{\pi}{4} - 8 \left( \frac{\pi}{4} \right) \quad \text{(31)}
$$

$$
= 6\pi - 2\pi \quad \text{(32)}
$$

$$
= 4\pi \quad \text{(33)}
$$

In (31) to (32) we noted that sin(3π/4) = sin(π/4).

So, we’ve noted again that the double integral over R is often simpler than the multi component line integral (often, but not always). We’ve also noted that Green’s The orem is independent of coordinate system, and we should continue to move back and forth between Cartesian and polar as needed.

**Example 3: "Green's Theorem Cannot Always Be Applied**.

- We need to go back and remember what we said when we first introduced Green’s Theorem- because there were a couple caveats (well, basically one caveat that applied to all functions involved in the expression).

“Green’s Theorem in the Plane”: Let C be a piecewise-smooth simple curve bounding a simply connected region R. If P(x,y), Q(x,y), ∂P/∂y and ∂Q/∂x are continuous on R, then:

$$
\oint_{C_+} [P(x, y) \, dx + Q(x, y) \, dy] = \iint_{R} \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right) dA. \quad \text{(34)}
$$

The key statement is the “If P(x,y), Q(x,y), ∂P/∂y and ∂Q/∂x are continuous on R...” part.

So that requirement means that we cannot have discontinuities in either the functions P or Q, and they must be smoothly varying (continuous first derivatives) along the two partial derivative directions indicated.

But here’s the more common problem- P,Q,∂P∂y,and∂Q∂x must all be defined within R. They can be zero- that’s fine. But they cannot go to ±∞. And that is something that comes up. It generally comes up when a denominator goes to zero (but there can be other cases). Let’s look at the following example.

- **Problem Statement**: Find the work done by ⃗ F acting along the simple closed curve C f igure in Figure 4.

$$\vec{F}={-y\over x^2+y^2}\hat{i}+{x\over x^2 + y^2}\hat{j}\tag{35}$$

![[Pasted image 20250203102017.png]]
**Figure 4: Contour and associated integration region for first example.**

So the region over which the functions need to be defined is the entire region contained within C. What happens to P and Q at the origin (i.e., at (x,y) = (0,0))? Well, they clearly go to infinity. So the requirements of Green’s Theorem are not met, and Green’s Theorem cannot be applied. You could still calculate the contour integral- no problem. But you’ll find that you cannot calculate the area integral. Calculating higher-order integrals over regions with “singularities” in them is a tricky business. Sometimes you can do it, sometimes the integral is not defined (the function is “not integrable” over that region). So we have to try to keep an eye out for these sorts of cases. Definitely if the functions involved are ratios of polynomials, we need to be aware of where the zeros of the denominator are. And probably the most common culprit (just in types of simpler functions that we deal a lot in courses like this) is the origin of the coordinate system. So watch out!
