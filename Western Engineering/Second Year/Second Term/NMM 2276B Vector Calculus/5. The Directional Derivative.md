
**This is about using the gradient operator to identify the rate of change of a function along specific directions**.

**Additionally...** will help realize how the gradient of a function gives the magnitude and direction of the most rapid rate of change of the function.

- The gradient operation yields the components of the spatial rate of change of a function F along each Cartesian direction;

$$\begin{equation}
\nabla F = \frac{\partial F}{\partial x} \hat{i} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} \hat{j} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial z} \hat{k}
\end{equation}
$$

- If we want the gradient along a specific Cartesian direction, we take just that component. **The formal mechanism is to take the dot-product of the gradient of $F$ with the component that we want** 

**Example, Formally extracting the $x$-component of the gradient:**

$$\begin{align}
(\nabla F) \cdot \hat{i} &= \left( \frac{\partial F}{\partial x} \hat{i} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} \hat{j} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial z} \hat{k} \right) \cdot \hat{i} \\
&= \frac{\partial F}{\partial x} (\hat{i} \cdot \hat{i}) + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} (\hat{j} \cdot \hat{i}) + \frac{\partial F}{\partial z} (\hat{k} \cdot \hat{i}) \\
&= \frac{\partial F}{\partial x} (1) + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} (0) + \frac{\partial F}{\partial z} (0) \\
&= \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}
\end{align}
$$

**In practice, we would write the following:**

$$(\nabla F) \cdot \hat{i} = \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}
$$
-> The proof is to show if we want the rate of change of a function (ie. the derivative) of a function $F$ along an arbitrary direction, we take the component of the gradient of $F$ along that direction.

**If we define the direction on which we want to find the derivative we use this formula with the unit-vector of the direction:**

$$\begin{equation}
D_{\hat{u}} F(x, y, z) = \nabla F \cdot \hat{u}
\end{equation}
$$

### Direction of Steepest Descent / Ascent

**Possible Question:** can we find the direction along which the gradient of F is the largest?

We proceed as follows. Let ⃗u be some arbitrary vector. The directional derivative along ⃗u is:

$$\begin{equation}
D_{\hat{u}} F(x, y, z) = \nabla F \cdot \hat{u}
\end{equation}
$$

The magnitude of this directional derivative is:

$$|D_{\hat{u}} F| = |\nabla F \cdot \hat{u}| = |\nabla F| \cdot |\hat{u}| \cdot \cos \phi
$$
where ϕ is the angle between ∇F and ˆ u. This angle must be between 0 and π, so cosϕ must be between −1 and +1. (Remember that |ˆ u| = 1.) Therefore we know that:

$$-|\nabla F| \leq D_{\mathbf{u}} F \leq |\nabla F|
$$

And so have an extremely important result: ∇F is a vector that points in the direction of the maximum rate of change of F. We often refer to this as the direction of steepest ascent (making analog again to the idea of the gradient of a height function). And for free, we also see that −∇F is the direction of the maximum rate of decrease of F (which we would call the direction of steepest descent)
