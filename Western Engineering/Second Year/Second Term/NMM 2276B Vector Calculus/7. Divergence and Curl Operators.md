
### 2D and 3D Vector Fields

- The definition of vector fields or vector functions over 2D and 3D space is relatively straightforward.

$$
\vec{F}(x, y) = P(x, y) \hat{i} + Q(x, y) \hat{j}
$$$$
\vec{F}(x, y, z) = P(x, y, z) \hat{i} + Q(x, y, z) \hat{j} + R(x, y, z) \hat{k}
$$
Pay attention to the notation. ⃗ F is a vector defined at every location in (x,y) or (x, y,z) as shown. The functions P,Q,R are scalar functions, defining the components of ⃗ F

Easy examples of 2D and 3D vector functions might be the electric field everywhere in space around some charge, or the magnetic field everywhere in space around some current.

- And were call that the gradient operator (Del) converts, via specific partial derivatives, a scalar function into an associated vector field: 

$$
\nabla = \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \right) \hat{i} + \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \right) \hat{j} + \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \right) \hat{k}
$$

$$
F(x, y, z) = \nabla \phi(x, y, z) = \left( \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} \right) \hat{i} + \left( \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} \right) \hat{j} + \left( \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z} \right) \hat{k}
$$

for some scalar function ϕ. Clearly ϕ and ⃗ F are highly related, and we are going to characterize and make use of that relationship a lot going forward.

### Divergence

- We define the "Divergence" as the following differential operation on a vector field. 

$$
\vec{F}(x, y, z) = P(x, y, z) \hat{i} + Q(x, y, z) \hat{j} + R(x, y, z) \hat{k} \tag{5}
$$

$$
\nabla \cdot \vec{F} \triangleq \left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \hat{i} + \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \hat{j} + \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \hat{k} \right] \cdot \left( P(x, y, z) \hat{i} + Q(x, y, z) \hat{j} + R(x, y, z) \hat{k} \right) \tag{6}
$$

$$
= \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z} \tag{7}
$$

It’s critical to recognize the difference in the form of the above result (Eq. 7) and the earlier gradient result (Eq. 4). The above is now a scalar function over \(\mathbb{R}^3\). So the divergence operation takes a vector function, operates on it via a special combination of partial differentials and a dot product, and yields a related scalar function. The gradient does something like the opposite (not that simple of course, but in a sense you can think of it that way).

### Curl

- We define the "Curl" as the following differential operation on a vector field:

$$
\vec{F}(x, y, z) = P(x, y, z) \hat{i} + Q(x, y, z) \hat{j} + R(x, y, z) \hat{k} \tag{5}
$$

$$
\nabla \times \vec{F} \triangleq \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \hat{i} + \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \hat{j} + \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \hat{k} \right) \times \left( P(x, y, z) \hat{i} + Q(x, y, z) \hat{j} + R(x, y, z) \hat{k} \right) \tag{6}
$$

$$
= \begin{vmatrix}
\hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\
\frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\
P & Q & R
\end{vmatrix} \tag{7}
$$

$$
= \hat{i} \left( \frac{\partial R}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z} \right) - \hat{j} \left( \frac{\partial R}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial z} \right) + \hat{k} \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right)
$$

And in this case, the curl operates on a vector field, and returns another vector field. Notice the pattern within the terms of the cross product (there is never a case where, for example, the Y component of the original vector field is operated on by a y partial differential).

### Couple General Results:

- There are several results (here we present 2 of them) that involve combinations of divergence and curl.

- The Curl of a Gradient of any continuous scalar function is always zero:
$$
\nabla \times (\nabla \phi) = 0
$$
- The Divergence of the Curl of any vector function is always zero: 
$$
\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \vec{F}) = 0
$$

So, what might the Curl of the Divergence of a vector field be? So, we need to think about it for a moment. We would need to start with a vector field \( \vec{F} \) (because the divergence only operates on vector fields). The result of the Divergence calculation is a scalar field. And there’s no way to take the curl of a scalar function. So actually, there’s no way we could take the Curl of the Divergence of a vector field! So, the question is a “trick” (or just a poorly formed question!).
