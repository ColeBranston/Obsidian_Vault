
### Gradient as Orthogonal to Level Curves and Surfaces

Let’s remember what we have so far. We have some function z = f(x,y), which we can think of (if we choose) as something like a “hill” over the x,y plane. Alevel curve will be the line for which f(x,y) = c for some constant c. We could choose many different values for c and obtain different level curves of f(x,y). An example of a level curve is a topological contour line for an altitude function. At any point P(x0,y0) on that level curve, ∇f(x0,y0) is orthogonal to the tangent vector to the level curve at that point. Note that ∇f(x0,y0) is not the gradient of f(x0,y0), but rather it is ∇f(x,y) evaluated at (x0,y0). So we say that ∇f is everywhere orthogonal to the level curve.

Figure 1: Schematic relationship between the gradient and a level curve at a point P(x0,y0).

- Extending this to 3D, a scalar function in 3D can be characterized by level surfaces, $F(x,y,z) = C$. Different values of $C$ would correspond to different level surfaces.

∇F is everywhere normal (i.e., perpendicular) to the level surface.

**Example:**

Consider level surface of F(x,y,z) = x2+y2+z2 that passes through the point (1,1,1). Find and plot the gradient at that point.

1. The scalar field:
$$
F(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2
$$

2. The gradient of the scalar field:
$$
\nabla F(x, y, z) = 2x \hat{i} + 2y \hat{j} + 2z \hat{k}
$$

3. The gradient at the point (1,1,1):
$$
\nabla F(1, 1, 1) = 2 \hat{i} + 2 \hat{j} + 2 \hat{k}
$$
What does this all look like? Well, the level surface of F(x,y,z) passing through (1,1,1) is actually a sphere of radius √3:

$$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1+1+1 = 3$$
The gradient points in the direction along which F is most rapidly changing, and it must be perpendicular to the level curves of the function. For our function, the vector perpendicular to the level curve (which is a sphere) must be a vector pointing straight away from the origin. And we see in Eq. 3 that this is indeed what we have

### Tangent Planes

**∇F can be used to define a tangent plane to a level surface**

We remember (just restating what we have above) that we are saying P(x0,y0,z0) is a point on F(x,y,z) = C, where ∇F= 0

The tangent plane at P is the plane through P that is normal to ∇F at P. Yes, okay, got it.

The equation for the plane tangent to P and perpendicular to ∇F is:

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial x} (x - x_0) + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} (y - y_0) + \frac{\partial F}{\partial z} (z - z_0) = 0
$$
In the above, we are saying that the dot-product of the gradient with a general position vector within the plane, is zero (and that defines that plane).

**Example:** Given $F=x^2-4y^2+z^2=16$ and a point (2,1,4) on that surface, find the equation for the tangent plane to F at that point

First thing- could check that (2,1,4) is actually on that surface

$$2^2 - 4(1^2) + 4^2 = 4 - 4 + 16 = 16
$$
Next we need the gradient of F at that point. So first we calculate the gradient function in general, then we evaluate the gradient function at the point of interest;

$$\nabla F = 2x \hat{i} - 8y \hat{j} + 2z \hat{k}
$$
$$\nabla F(2, 1, 4) = 4 \hat{i} - 8 \hat{j} + 8 \hat{k}
$$

-> Find the plane normal to the vector prev.

$$\begin{align}
\nabla F &= 2x \hat{i} - 8y \hat{j} + 2z \hat{k} \\
\nabla F(2, 1, 4) &= 4 \hat{i} - 8 \hat{j} + 8 \hat{k} \\
4(x - 2) - 8(y - 1) + 8(z - 4) &= 0 \\
4x - 8y + 8z &= 32
\end{align}
$$

and that is the equation for a plane (you could solve it for $z$ and obtain a first order polynomial for the plane as function of $(x,y)$).
