
**What Is Partial Differentiation**:

Given $z = f(x,y)$, we can see that there are two ways in which we could consider a derivative. We can ask how *f* varies with *x*, and separately how *f* varies with *y*. In each case, we would consider the other variable to be held constant while we examine the local function slope. 

Partial derivative of *f* with respect to *x* is defined in the same manner the ordinary derivative was defined - in terms o fa slope, in the limit that the step size goes to zero:

$${\partial f \over \partial x} = \underset{\Delta x\rightarrow0}{lim}{f(x+\Delta x, y) - f(x,y) \over \Delta x}$$

and similarly the partial derivative of *f* with respect to *y* is defined as:

$${\partial f \over \partial y} = \underset{\Delta y \rightarrow0}{lim}{f(x,y+\Delta y) - f(x,y) \over \Delta y}$$

**Side Note:** these definitions of the derivative as a direct estimation of the change in a function over a small interval step may seem unimportant - once we make these definitions, we rarely go back to them except to derive some properties of derivatives, etc. But when you get into the numerical solution of differential equations (DEs), you will find that we actually implement these very forms in calculating the derivative terms and factors in real DEs (both ordinary and partial).

- Because in each partial derivative, we treat the other variable or variables as constant, the effect is that the rules of partial differentiation are exactly those of ordinary differentiation (again, as long as you keep the other variables as effectively constant). So really partial differentiation is very easy based on what you already know.

Example:

**Let f(x,y) = 4x3y2 − 4x2 + y6 +1. Find the partial derivatives of f with respect to both x and y** 

$${\partial f \over \partial x} = {\partial\over \partial x}(4x^3y^2 - 4x^2+y^6+1)$$
$$=4y^2{\partial \over \partial x}(x^3) - 4{\partial \over \partial x}(x^2)+0+0$$$$=4y^2(3x^2) - 4(2x)$$
$$=12x^2y^2 - 8x$$
<center> and </center>
$$={\partial f\over\partial y} = {\partial \over \partial y}(4x^3y^2-4x^2+y^6+1)$$
$$=4x^3{\partial \over \partial y}(y^2)-0+{\partial\over \partial y}(y^6) + 0$$
$$=4x^3(2y)+6y^5$$
$$=8x^3y+6y^5$$
- It's as easy as that. Partial differentiation, like ordinary differentiation, is only complicate to the extent that the functions are complicated (usually the chain rule is about the most powerful thing we need to break-out to handle most any problem).

- The practice of higher-order differentials is equally easy to understand, and the same as what you have seen before with ordinary differentiation. You just keep repeating the process, keeping whatever variables are not part of the current differentiation process constant

## Various Nomenclature & Higher Order Differentials


- There is a short-hand we use sometimes in differential equations:

$$f_x \triangleq \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \text{ and } f_y \triangleq \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}
$$
- We will see higher order partial derivatives, and they require additional notation:

$$\begin{align*}
f_{xx} &\triangleq \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \right) \triangleq \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} \\
f_{yy} &\triangleq \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \right) \triangleq \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} \\
f_{xy} &\triangleq \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \right) \triangleq \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y} \\
f_{yx} &\triangleq \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y}
\end{align*}
$$

In the last equality we are noting that the order of differentiation in the higher order "mixed" derivatives does not matter.

![[Chain Rule]]

There is a special case here, and that would be the case where z = f(u,v), but now we have parameterized them such that u = u(t) and v = v(t) (think about the curve parameterization problems we’ve started to look at):

$$\begin{equation}
\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial u} \frac{du}{dt} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} \frac{dv}{dt}
\end{equation}
$$

Again, nothing special about parameterizing the functions with t (but we do use it a lot- my point is just that we don’t have to!

Okay, so one final thing to maybe discuss or clarify at this point, since this often causes some confusion: why do I sometimes say z(x,y)z(x,y) and other times I say z=f(x,y)z = f(x,y)? The short answer is, it doesn't matter. But that answer probably doesn't help much.

Here's what I mean: we have a function of xx and yy (just talking about the 2D case here), and that function is f(x,y)f(x,y). We like to, and find it very helpful to, think about f(x,y)f(x,y) as being "above" the (x,y)(x,y) plane, and in that sense, we can consider f(x,y)f(x,y) as being represented by zz. Just think about 1D calculus from last year.

Sometimes you would say f(x)f(x), and other times you would say yy or y(x)y(x). It doesn't matter, as long as you are clear in your own head. What starts to happen though (and really, it happens very soon, as soon as we're dealing with functions of 3 variables, like temperature throughout the 3D volume of this classroom), is that we no longer have an obvious variable to consider equal to the value of the function. So, we start to rely more heavily on the f(x,y,z)f(x,y,z) notation, and we will stop trying to say anything like w=f(x,y,z)w = f(x,y,z). Not that it would be wrong to do so, it just stops being helpful.
