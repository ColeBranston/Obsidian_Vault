
# Conductors
![[Conductors]]

**In electrostatic equilibrium, the electric field inside a conductor is zero**
<br>![[Pasted image 20240124111645.png]]
<br>
- In electrostatic equilibrium the charge distribution does not change with time, so the charges don’t move. 
- Imagine if there were an electric field inside the conductor. 
	- That field would exert a force on the electrons, causing them to move: contradicting our assumption of electrostatic equilibrium.
	- Hence the electric field inside the conductor must be zero
<br>
![[Pasted image 20240124113542.png]]
<br>
- If the external field changes, the conduction electrons move around until they reach equilibrium, at which point they stop moving.
- If the electrons are not moving, then there is no net force acting on them, so no field.
- In the figure to the right, the electric field inside both conductors is zero!

**Because the field inside a conductor is zero, the entire volume of the conductor is at the same potential**

- Remember that:

$$E= (-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}i,-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}j, -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}k)$$

- If 𝐸 is zero inside the conductor, then each of these derivatives must be zero, and so V is constant inside the conductor.

![[Pasted image 20240124121250.png]]

**The field at the surface of a conductor in equilibrium is normal to the surface.**

![[Pasted image 20240124121912.png]]

- If the electric field at the surface of a conductor had a component parallel to the surface, then the electrons would move in response to the field. 
- However, in electrostatic equilibrium, the electrons don’t move: 
	- so the component parallel to the surface must be zero. 
	- The electric field is therefore normal to the surface.

# Conductors and Electric Potential

![[Pasted image 20240124122041.png]]

We have just argued that the [[Electric Field Lines]] at the surface of a conductor in equilibrium must be perpendicular to the surface of the conductor → But we know that electric field lines are perpendicular to equipotential surfaces. → Therefore, the surface of a conductor must be an equipotential surface.

**The magnitude of the electric field at the surface of a conductor is proportional to the surface charge density.**

- It can be shown that the field at the surface of a conductor with a surface charge density 𝜎 is:

$$E = 2\pi k \sigma = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}$$

- As we’ve seen, this field is directed perpendicular to the surface of the conductor, either outward (if the conductor is positively charged) or inward (if it’s negatively charged).

**The magnitude of the electric field at the surface of a conductor increases as the radius of curvature gets smaller.**

- Consider the two points P and P’ shown on the surface of the conductor.

$$R_p > R_{p'} \text{, so }  E_P < E_{p'} $$

**Conductors in electrostatic equilibrium: Summary**

- The electric field inside a conductor is zero everywhere.
- The entire volume of the conductor is at the same potential. 
- There is no net charge in the interior of a conductor, any excess charge resides on the surface. 
- The field at the surface of the conductor is perpendicular to the surface of the conductor.
- The surface of a conductor is an equipotential surface. 
- The magnitude of the field at the surface is proportional to the surface charge density. 
- The magnitude of the electric field at the surface increases as the radius of curvature gets smaller.
