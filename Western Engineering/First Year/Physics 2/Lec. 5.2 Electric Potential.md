
**Example**

- The two terminals of a battery are at different electric potentials.
- The potential difference, i.e., [[Voltage]] (potential of positive terminal minus potential of negative terminal) in the case shown is 1.5 volts (V).
- A potential difference creates an electric field that can drive an electric current.

![[Pasted image 20240118133919.png]]

- The *electric potential,* V is defined as the electric potential energy U per unit charge. 

- Using a test charge $q_0$ in the electric field of a point charge q , we find that the electric potential V of the point charge q is: 

$$V = \frac{U}{q_0} = \frac{kq}{r}$$

- Conversely, a charge $q_0$ sitting at a point where the electric potential is V will have an electric potential energy given by:

$$U = q_0 V$$

**Electric Potential and Electric Potential Energy are not the Same!**

- The relationship between U and V is similar to the relationship between 𝐹 ⃗ and 𝐸. 
- U and V are scalars, while the force and field are vectors. Scalars are easier to deal with than vectors! 
- Just as with potential energy, it is *differences* in electric potential that are important, not the value of the potential itself. Potential is always measured relative to the potential at some reference point.

# Electric Potential

- The units of electric potential (and potential difference) are J/C = volts (V)
- The voltage ∆𝑽 (or $V_{ab}$ on the figure to the right) of a battery tells you how much [[Work]] *W* the battery does on each coulomb of charge that passes through it.

$$W = \Delta U = q \Delta V$$
- A positive charge $q_0$ will “want” to move from regions of higher potential to regions of lower potential to decrease its potential energy. 
	- which is how we get electric current.
