**Electric Field From Continuous Charge Distributions**

- Consider an arbitrary object with total charge Q. 
- The number of electrons that make up the charge Q can be huge, so it is impractical treat each charge individually. 
- We can treat the charge on the object as being “smeared out” and continuously distributed throughout the object.
- How do we calculate [[Lec. 2.2 The Electric Field]] generated by a continuous charge distribution? 
- Divide the total volume up into N small volumes, each containing charge Dq. 
- The total charge Q is given by adding up the charge in all these small volumes.

$$Q=\sum_{i=1}^N{\Delta q_i}$$
- If we let N go to infinity, then ∆𝑞𝑖 → 𝑑𝑞 and the sum becomes an [[Integral]]. The total charge is

$$Q=\lim_{n → \infty} \sum_{i=1}^N{\Delta q_i}$$
$$\int dq$$

- We can calculate the field due to each element of charge Dqi in a similar way. The field at point P due to the element of charge Dq is given by

$$\Delta E = \frac{k\Delta q}{r^2}r$$
- The total field at P is calculated by summing over all the elements of charge:

$$E=k\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\Delta q_i}{r_i^2}r_i$$![[Pasted image 20240112105711.png]]

- In the limit when N goes to infinity, the sum becomes an [[Integral]]. The total field at point P due to the entire charge distribution is given by:

$$E=k\lim_{N → \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\Delta q_i}{r_i^2}r_i$$
$$=k\int \frac{dq}{r^2} r_i$$

**Example: Electric Field at the end of a Finite Charged Wire**

Let’s consider a simple case where the charge Q is uniformly distributed along a line, i.e., a length of wire of length l. Say we want to find [[Lec. 2.2 The Electric Field]] at point P on the x-axis. We can divide the wire into many small segments. We can then treat each small segment as a point charge and calculate [[Lec. 2.2 The Electric Field]] at P due to each segment. To get the total field we will then have to add up all these small contributions.

**Critical Step 1:** We first define the linear charge density $\lambda$ as

$$\lambda=Q/l$$
We divide the rod up into N segments of length Dx. Each segment has a charge $\Delta q$ given by: 
$$\Delta q=\lambda \Delta x$$
![[Pasted image 20240112112551.png]]
If N is sufficiently large, then we can treat each segment as a point charge $\Delta q_i$. [[Lec. 2.2 The Electric Field]] at point *P* due to the charge $\Delta q_i$ is then given by:
$$\Delta E_i = k \frac{\Delta q_i}{x_i ^2} r$$

The total electric field at *P* due to the entire rod is obtained by adding up the contributions due to all of the segments.

This is still not very useful. We would like to express this sum in terms of a quantity over which we can integrate, such as a length interval ∆𝑥

$$E = \sum _{i=1}^N \Delta E_i$$
$$=k \sum _{i=1} ^{N} \frac{\Delta q_i}{x_i^2} r$$
**Critical Step 2:** 

To do this, we note that the charge on each segment $\Delta q_i$ is given by:

$$\Delta q_i = \lambda \Delta x$$
									and that 
	$$r_i =-i$$
[[Lec. 2.2 The Electric Field]] can then be written as: 

$$E=k\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{\Delta q_i}{q_i ^2} i$$
$$=-k\sum _{i=1} ^{N} \frac{\lambda \Delta x}{x_i ^2}i$$
$$=-k \lambda (\sum _{i=1} ^{N} \frac{\Delta x}{x_i ^2})i$$
[[Lec. 2.2 The Electric Field]] at point *P* due to the rod is then given by:

$$=-k \lambda (\sum _{i=1} ^{N} \frac{\Delta x}{x_i ^2})i$$
**Critical Step 3:**

Now we make the number of segments N very large. In the limit when N goes to infinity, ∆𝑥→𝑑𝑥 and the sum over N becomes an [[Integral]] over the length of the wire:

$$=-k \lambda (\sum _{i=1} ^{N} \frac{\Delta x}{x_i ^2})i$$
$$=-k \lambda (\int _a ^{a+1} \frac{dx}{x^2})i$$

Reminder: 

![[Integral]]

$$\therefore \lim_{N → \infty} \sum _{i=1} ^{N} \frac{\Delta x_i}{x_i ^2} = \int _{x_{min}} ^{x^{max}} \frac{dx}{x^2} $$

Now we have to evaluate the [[Integral]]...

$$\int _a ^{l+a} \frac{dx}{x^2} = -\frac{1}{x} | _a ^{l+a}$$

Same Picture as before:
![[Pasted image 20240114212412.png]] 

Now we can determine the electric field at P due to the entire rod. It is:

$$E = -k \lambda (\int _a ^{a+1} \frac{dx}{x^2})i$$
$$=-k \lambda(\frac{l}{a(l+a)})i$$
$$=-k(\frac{\lambda l}{a(l+a)})i$$

But $\lambda l = Q$, so...

$$E=-k \frac{\lambda l}{a(l+a)} i$$
$$=-k \frac{Q}{a(l+a)} i$$
**Example: Electric field at the end of a finite charged wire**

So here is our result at last. The field at point P due to the charged wire is: Reality check: if the length l goes to zero we find: which is just the field due to a point charge at x = a !

$$E=-k \frac{Q}{a(l+a)} i$$
$$E=-k\frac{Q}{a^2} i$$

**This is how we would calculate the electric field from a charged rod**

![[Pasted image 20240114213155.png]]
$$E=-k \frac{Q}{a(l+a)} i$$

- Using the integration approach, we can calculate the electric field of anything! …at least in principle.
