A **formal language** over an [[Alphabet]] Σ, written L, is a subset L ⊆ Σ* (see [[Kleene Star]]). It is, plainly, a set of [[String]]s. The interesting ones are those produced by some **rule**.

Over Σ = {0, 1}:

| The set | The rule |
|---|---|
| {0, 10, 00, 000, 010, 100, 110, …} | strings that end in 0 |
| {1, 01, 10, 11, 001, 010, 011, 100, …} | strings with at least one 1 |

**Formal vs. natural language**

*Differences* — in a formal language membership is **exact**: every string either is in L or is not. No ambiguity, no context, no exceptions. English is ambiguous, context-dependent, and its rules shift over time.

*Similarities* — both are sets of strings over an alphabet, and in both cases the interesting ones are generated by rules. For natural languages those rules are a grammar; formal grammars arrive in Week 5.

This definition is what makes [[Decision Problem]]s possible: computation becomes the act of deciding whether s ∈ L.
